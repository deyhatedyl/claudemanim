## [00:59] E03S02 The axes and the two endpoints

An energy profile has enthalpy on the vertical axis. The horizontal axis is called the reaction coordinate, or reaction progress. It is not time. It shows how far the atoms have got in rearranging from reactants into products, so a profile says nothing about how fast a reaction goes.
Start every profile with the two endpoints. Draw a flat level for the reactants on the left, and a flat level for the products on the right. For an exothermic reaction, the products sit lower.
The vertical gap between the two levels is delta H. Measured from reactants down to products, it's negative here. Notice that delta H only depends on where the two endpoints sit. We haven't drawn anything in between yet, and we don't need to in order to know delta H.

## [01:57] E03S03 Activation energy, forward and reverse

Now join the endpoints with a curve. To get from reactants to products, bonds must start breaking before new ones fully form, so the energy first rises to a maximum, called the transition state, and then falls to the products.
The activation energy is the minimum energy needed to reach the transition state from the reactants. On the graph, it's the vertical distance from the reactant level up to the peak. That's the forward activation energy.
Run the reaction backwards, from products to reactants, and you climb from the product level up to the same peak. That's the reverse activation energy. For an exothermic reaction it's bigger than the forward one, because the products start lower.
So there are three vertical distances, and every one of them is a difference between two heights. Forward activation energy: peak minus reactants. Reverse activation energy: peak minus products. Delta H: products minus reactants. None of them is read straight off the axis.
For an endothermic reaction the picture flips. The products sit above the reactants, so delta H is positive, and the forward activation energy is the larger of the two.

## [03:21] E03S04 What a catalyst changes, and what it doesn't

A catalyst provides an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy. On the profile, that's a second curve with a lower peak. Let's draw it as a dashed line over the original.
Look at the endpoints. The catalysed path starts at the same reactant level and finishes at the same product level. The catalyst is not consumed, and it doesn't change what the reactants and products are, so their enthalpies don't change.
So the catalyst lowers the forward activation energy, and the reverse activation energy by the same amount. But delta H is exactly the same.
Checkpoint. A student says: "Adding a catalyst makes an exothermic reaction release more heat." Is that right? Pause and decide, with a reason.  *(pause)*
No. For the same amount reacting, the heat released equals the size of delta H, and the catalyst doesn't change delta H. The reaction may reach the same result faster, but the energy released for that amount is unchanged.

## [06:59] E03S06 Thermochemical equations: scale and reverse

A thermochemical equation is a balanced equation with a delta H attached. Here's hydrogen burning to form liquid water: delta H equals negative five hundred and seventy-two kilojoules. That value refers to the equation as written: two moles of hydrogen reacting with one mole of oxygen to form two moles of liquid water.
If you double the equation, you double the amounts reacting, so you double delta H. Four moles of hydrogen burning releases twice as much energy: delta H becomes negative one thousand one hundred and forty-four kilojoules. The coefficients and delta H always scale together.
If you reverse the equation, the same energy change runs the other way, so the sign flips. Decomposing two moles of liquid water into hydrogen and oxygen has delta H equal to positive five hundred and seventy-two kilojoules. It takes as much energy to pull water apart as was released when it formed.
Sometimes you want a molar quantity for one substance. Dividing the equation by two gives one mole of hydrogen forming one mole of water, with delta H equal to negative two hundred and eighty-six kilojoules. So the molar enthalpy of combustion of hydrogen is negative two hundred and eighty-six kilojoules per mole of hydrogen. Always say which substance "per mole" refers to.

## [08:32] E03S07 States matter: liquid or gaseous water

Look closely at the states in that equation. The water is a liquid. What if the product were water vapour instead?
Turning liquid water into vapour absorbs energy: about forty-four kilojoules per mole at twenty-five degrees. So gaseous water sits higher in enthalpy than the same amount of liquid water.
That means forming two moles of water vapour releases less energy than forming two moles of liquid water: about four hundred and eighty-four kilojoules instead of five hundred and seventy-two. You can't reuse negative five hundred and seventy-two if the product state changes. You need data for the states actually involved.
This is why states are never decoration in a thermochemical equation. Hot exhaust gases contain water vapour, while a calorimeter at room temperature mostly ends up with liquid water. Which value applies depends on the states.

## [11:30] E03S09 Recap and closing recall

To recap. On an energy profile, every quantity is a vertical difference: forward activation energy, reverse activation energy and delta H. A catalyst lowers the peak but leaves both endpoints, and delta H, unchanged. In a thermochemical equation, delta H scales with the coefficients and changes sign when the equation is reversed, and it depends on the states.
