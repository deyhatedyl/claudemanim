## [01:04] E08S02 Where the energy goes

Here's a common way to measure the energy from a fuel. A spirit burner holds the liquid fuel. Above it, a metal can holds a measured mass of water, with a thermometer to track its temperature.
When the fuel burns, the energy it releases goes several places. Some heats the water: that's the useful energy we can measure. Some heats the can and the thermometer. And a lot simply heats the surrounding air, especially the hot gases rising past the sides of the can.
There's another loss too. In a small flame, combustion is often incomplete. You may see soot on the bottom of the can. Incompletely burned fuel releases less energy than the equation for complete combustion predicts.
So the energy gained by the water is always less than the energy the fuel could release. Our job is to measure that useful part, and compare it with the total.

## [02:11] E08S03 Building q = mc delta T

Start small. To warm one gram of water by one degree Celsius takes four point one eight joules. That number is the specific heat capacity of water, c: four point one eight joules per gram per degree.
Two grams need twice as much energy for the same one-degree rise, and two hundred and fifty grams need two hundred and fifty times as much. So energy is proportional to the mass of water.
And warming the same water by ten degrees instead of one takes ten times the energy. So energy is also proportional to the temperature rise.
Put those together: q equals m times c times delta T. Check the units: grams, times joules per gram per degree, times degrees. Grams cancel, degrees cancel, leaving joules.
Now the important questions. Whose mass? The water's, because the water is what's being heated, not the fuel. And delta T is the temperature rise, final minus initial, not the final temperature itself.

## [03:22] E08S04 Mass and energy change the temperature rise

Rearranging gives delta T equals q divided by m c. Let's see what that predicts. Put four thousand one hundred and eighty joules into one hundred grams of water: the temperature rises ten degrees.
Put the same energy into two hundred grams of water: the rise halves, to five degrees. The energy is spread over twice as much water.
Now double the energy, to eight thousand three hundred and sixty joules, into the original one hundred grams: the rise doubles, to twenty degrees. Keep these two effects in mind whenever a question changes the mass of water or the amount of fuel.

## [04:08] E08S05 How much fuel burned? A mass reading

To know how much fuel burned, weigh the burner before and after heating. The mass lost is the mass of fuel burned, assuming nothing else escaped. Divide by molar mass to get the amount in moles, then multiply by the molar enthalpy of combustion to get the energy the fuel released.
The same idea measures energy in food. Suppose one point five zero grams of dry food burns under a can holding one hundred point zero grams of water, and the water warms by eight point zero degrees. The water gains one hundred times four point one eight times eight point zero: three thousand three hundred and forty-four joules.
Per gram of food, that's three thousand three hundred and forty-four divided by one point five zero: about two point two kilojoules per gram. That's far less than a typical nutrition label value of fifteen or more kilojoules per gram for a dry, carbohydrate-rich food.
Two reasons. First, most of the energy escaped to the can and the air, and some food probably didn't burn completely. Second, a label reports the energy the body can obtain from food, which is a different basis from heat measured in a can. So call this result an experimental water-heat yield, and don't treat it as the food's label energy.

## [07:56] E08S07 Efficiency, forwards and backwards

Efficiency compares what you get with what you put in. Efficiency equals useful energy output divided by total energy input, times one hundred percent. Always say what counts as useful, and keep both energies in the same unit.
Sometimes you know the useful energy you need, and the efficiency, and you want the input. Rearrange: total input equals useful output divided by the efficiency, written as a fraction, like zero point four five zero.
Test it with limiting cases. At one hundred percent efficiency, the input would just equal the useful output. At forty-five percent, dividing by zero point four five zero makes the input more than twice as big. A less efficient heater needs more fuel to deliver the same useful energy. Dividing by a number less than one makes the answer larger, which is exactly what should happen.

Too small. Multiplying by zero point four five zero gives less than the useful output itself, which would mean getting more energy out than you put in. That's impossible.
