## [01:32] E01S02 One container, several different labels

Picture a flask of liquid ethanol. We can describe the same sample in several ways. Its mass, in grams, is what a balance reads. Its volume, in millilitres or litres, is how much space it takes up. And its amount, in moles, is a count of how many particles it contains.
A mole is a counting unit, like a dozen, but enormous: about six point zero two times ten to the power of twenty-three particles. Chemists count in moles because reactions happen particle by particle, so the recipe of a reaction is written in particles, and therefore in moles.
Molar mass links mass to amount. It is the mass of one mole of a substance, in grams per mole. For ethanol it's forty-six point zero grams per mole, so forty-six grams of ethanol is exactly one mole.
Now let's swap the ethanol for the same mass of water: forty-six grams again. The mass label is unchanged. But water molecules are lighter, at eighteen grams per mole, so the same forty-six grams contains about two point five six moles. Same mass, different amount.
So these labels are not interchangeable. You cannot replace grams with moles, or millilitres with litres, without a relationship that connects them. The next scene gives you those relationships.

## [03:05] E01S03 Three ways to get moles, and why the units cancel

First, from a mass. Amount in moles equals mass divided by molar mass. Watch the units. Mass is in grams. Molar mass is in grams per mole. Dividing grams by grams per mole cancels the grams and leaves moles. The units tell you the relationship is set up correctly.
Second, from a solution. Amount equals concentration times volume. Concentration is in moles per litre, so the volume must be in litres. Then litres cancel, and you're left with moles.
Third, from a gas. At standard laboratory conditions, which in this course means twenty-five degrees Celsius and one hundred kilopascals, one mole of any gas occupies about twenty-four point eight litres. This is the molar volume. Amount equals gas volume divided by molar volume: litres divided by litres per mole gives moles.
Two warnings about that last one. It applies only to gases, and only at those stated conditions. A hot exhaust gas at six hundred degrees takes up far more space per mole, so twenty-four point eight would give the wrong answer there.
Now a checkpoint. A solution has a concentration of zero point one zero zero moles per litre, and you have two hundred and fifty millilitres of it. Why can't you multiply zero point one zero zero by two hundred and fifty? Pause and explain it in your own words.  *(pause)*
Because the concentration is per litre, the volume must be in litres for the units to cancel. Two hundred and fifty millilitres is zero point two five zero litres. Multiplying gives zero point zero two five zero moles. If you multiply by two hundred and fifty instead, you get twenty-five, which is a thousand times too big. The units left over, millilitres times moles per litre, don't simplify to moles. That mismatch is your warning sign.

## [05:25] E01S04 The unit ladder

Unit conversions cause more lost marks than difficult chemistry, so let's make them mechanical. Here is a volume ladder. One cubic metre is one thousand litres, and one litre is one thousand millilitres. Each step down the ladder multiplies by a thousand.
Here's a direction rule. When you move to a smaller unit, you need more of them, so the number gets bigger. When you move to a larger unit, the number gets smaller. For example, zero point zero eight five zero cubic metres becomes eighty-five point zero litres, and then eighty-five thousand millilitres.
Mass works the same way. One kilogram is one thousand grams, and one gram is one thousand milligrams. Energy too: one megajoule is one thousand kilojoules, and one kilojoule is one thousand joules. So seventy-two thousand joules is seventy-two point zero kilojoules.
Time is the odd one out, because its steps are sixty, not a thousand. One hour is sixty minutes, and one minute is sixty seconds, so one hour is three thousand six hundred seconds. Zero point one five zero hours is nine minutes, which is five hundred and forty seconds.
After every conversion, ask one question: should the number have got bigger or smaller? If it went the wrong way, you multiplied when you should have divided.

## [06:59] E01S05 A balanced equation is a recipe in moles

A balanced equation is a recipe. Hydrogen burning in oxygen: two hydrogen molecules react with one oxygen molecule to make two water molecules. Count the atoms on each side. Four hydrogen atoms and two oxygen atoms before, and the same after. Nothing appears or disappears; the atoms are just rearranged.
Now scale the recipe up. Make it ten times bigger, or a million times bigger, and the ratio stays two to one to two. Scale it up by a mole, and the coefficients become amounts: two moles of hydrogen react with one mole of oxygen to make two moles of water.
So a coefficient gives a mole ratio. If zero point four zero moles of hydrogen reacts, it needs half as much oxygen, zero point two zero moles, and it makes zero point four zero moles of water. The ratio is valid because it comes from counting particles, and moles are a count.
But the coefficients are not a mass ratio. Zero point four zero moles of hydrogen has a mass of only zero point eight grams, while zero point two zero moles of oxygen has a mass of six point four grams. Mass is still conserved: zero point eight plus six point four gives seven point two grams of water. But the masses are not in a two to one to two ratio. Use the equation with moles, then convert to grams at the end.

## [08:42] E01S06 Round at the end, not along the way

One last tool before the questions: rounding. Suppose an oxygen volume works out to seventeen point seven six five litres, and you need the amount in moles. If you round that volume to seventeen point eight first, then divide by twenty-four point eight, you get zero point seven one eight moles.
If you keep the full value instead, you get zero point seven one six three, which rounds to zero point seven one six moles. The early rounding changed the third significant figure. In a longer calculation, those small errors build up.
So keep unrounded values in your calculator, write the intermediate result with a few extra digits, and round only the final answer. Base the number of significant figures on the data you were given, which here is three.
