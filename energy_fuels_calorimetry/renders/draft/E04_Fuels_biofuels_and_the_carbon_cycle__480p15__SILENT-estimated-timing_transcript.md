## [01:02] E04S02 What counts as a fuel: the fossil fuels

A fuel is a substance that undergoes a chemical reaction, usually combustion with oxygen, to release useful energy, most often as heat. The useful part matters: we choose fuels because the energy can be captured and used.
The fossil fuels are coal, natural gas and petrol. Coal is mostly carbon. Natural gas is mostly methane. Petrol is a mixture of liquid hydrocarbons refined from crude oil, such as octane.
Fossil fuels formed from the remains of organisms buried and transformed by heat and pressure over millions of years. We're using them far faster than they can form, so on any human timescale they are non-renewable.

## [01:49] E04S03 Renewable means replenished on a human timescale

Compare two timescales. A fossil fuel took millions of years to form. A biofuel is made from biomass, such as crops, plant waste or animal fat, that can be regrown or replaced within months or years. A fuel is renewable if it is replenished at least as fast as we use it.
There are three biofuels to know. Biogas is a mixture of mainly methane and carbon dioxide, made when microbes break down organic waste without oxygen. Bioethanol is ethanol made by fermenting sugars. And biodiesel is made by reacting plant oils or animal fats with an alcohol, in a process called transesterification.
Notice something important. Methane from biogas and methane from natural gas are the same molecule. Burning one mole of either releases the same energy and forms the same carbon dioxide. What differs is where the carbon came from, and how quickly it can be replaced.

## [02:54] E04S04 Following the carbon atoms

Let's follow some carbon atoms. They start in carbon dioxide in the atmosphere. During photosynthesis, a plant uses energy from sunlight to build those carbon atoms into glucose.
The glucose can be fermented into ethanol. The same carbon atoms are now in the fuel, with some leaving as carbon dioxide during fermentation.
When the ethanol burns, its carbon atoms return to the atmosphere as carbon dioxide, and the next crop can take them up again. Over a full cycle, this carbon is recycled on a timescale of months or years.
Burning a fossil fuel is different. Its carbon was locked away underground for millions of years. Releasing it adds extra carbon dioxide to the atmosphere, faster than natural processes can remove it.
But careful: the biofuel cycle isn't automatically carbon neutral. Growing, harvesting, processing and transporting the fuel can use fossil energy too. We'll look at those wider boundaries in episode twelve.

## [04:02] E04S05 Photosynthesis and respiration

Photosynthesis: six carbon dioxide plus six water forms one glucose plus six oxygen. Check the atoms: six carbons, twelve hydrogens, and eighteen oxygens on each side.
Photosynthesis is endothermic. The products, glucose and oxygen, sit higher in enthalpy than carbon dioxide and water. That energy doesn't appear from nowhere: it is absorbed from sunlight. Light energy is converted into chemical energy.
Cellular respiration is the reverse overall change. Glucose reacts with oxygen to form carbon dioxide and water, releasing roughly two point eight thousand kilojoules per mole of glucose. As we saw in episode two, that energy is released because the bonds formed in carbon dioxide and water are stronger, not because glucose's bonds break.

## [04:53] E04S06 Making bioethanol: fermentation and distillation

Fermentation: glucose in solution forms two ethanol and two carbon dioxide. Watch the atoms. Six carbons, twelve hydrogens and six oxygens go in, and exactly the same atoms come out, just rearranged.
This reaction is catalysed by enzymes from yeast, in the absence of oxygen, at a warm temperature, around thirty to thirty-five degrees. Enzymes are proteins with a specific shape. At a very high temperature, their shape is permanently damaged. We say the enzyme is denatured, and the fermentation slows or stops.
Fermentation only produces a dilute ethanol solution, around ten to fifteen percent ethanol, because the yeast can't survive higher concentrations. So we separate and concentrate the ethanol by distillation.
Distillation uses the difference in boiling points. Ethanol boils at about seventy-eight degrees, water at one hundred. When the mixture is heated, the vapour is richer in ethanol than the liquid, and it's cooled and collected in a condenser. The distillate is much more concentrated, but ordinary distillation can't produce pure ethanol: it reaches about ninety-five percent at most.

## [08:53] E04S08 Biogas and biodiesel

Biogas comes from anaerobic digestion. Organic waste, like manure, food scraps or sewage, is sealed in a tank without oxygen. Microbes break it down over days to weeks, producing a gas that's mainly methane and carbon dioxide. The leftover solid, called digestate, can be used as fertiliser.
Biodiesel starts from fats and oils, which are triglycerides. A triglyceride is three fatty acid chains attached to a glycerol backbone by ester links.
In transesterification, the triglyceride reacts with three molecules of an alcohol, usually methanol, with a catalyst such as potassium hydroxide. Each fatty acid chain swaps partners: it leaves the glycerol and attaches to the alcohol. So one triglyceride plus three alcohol molecules gives three fatty acid methyl esters, which are the biodiesel, plus one glycerol.
For this series, that ratio is the key: one triglyceride, three alcohol, three esters, one glycerol. The detailed organic chemistry belongs to the organic chemistry topic; here we just need to recognise the reaction and why the product is a renewable fuel.
