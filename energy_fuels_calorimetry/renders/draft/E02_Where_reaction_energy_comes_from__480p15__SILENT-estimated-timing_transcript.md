## [01:17] E02S02 System, surroundings, heat and temperature

To talk about energy changes precisely, we split the world in two. The system is the reacting chemicals themselves, the substances whose bonds are changing. Everything else is the surroundings: the water they're dissolved in, the cup, the thermometer, the air.
When a reaction happens, energy can be transferred across the boundary between the system and the surroundings. Energy transferred because of a temperature difference is called heat. So heat is not a substance that flows around. It's a name for energy on the move.
Temperature is different. Temperature measures how hot something is, which reflects the average kinetic energy of its particles. It is not the total amount of energy something contains.
Here's an example. A cup of tea at eighty degrees is hotter than a bath at forty degrees. But the bath contains far more water, so it holds far more thermal energy. Higher temperature does not mean more energy. In calorimetry we'll use temperature changes to work out how much heat was transferred.

## [02:30] E02S03 Exothermic and endothermic

In an exothermic reaction, energy is transferred from the system to the surroundings. The surroundings gain that energy, so the water warms up and the thermometer reading rises. Combustion of a fuel is exothermic.
In an endothermic reaction, energy is transferred from the surroundings into the system. The surroundings lose energy, so the water cools and the thermometer reading falls. An instant cold pack is a familiar example.
We can attach signs to these transfers. Energy gained counts as positive, and energy lost counts as negative. In an exothermic reaction the system loses energy, so q for the system is negative, while q for the surroundings is positive.
If no energy escapes anywhere else, these two are equal in size and opposite in sign. Whatever the system loses, the surroundings gain. That single idea, energy is conserved and only moves, underpins every calorimetry calculation in this series.

## [03:36] E02S04 Breaking bonds absorbs energy; making bonds releases it

So where does the energy come from? Start with a single hydrogen molecule. The two atoms are held together by a bond, an attraction between the nuclei and the shared electrons. To pull the atoms apart, you have to supply energy. Breaking a bond always absorbs energy.
Now run it the other way. When two separate hydrogen atoms come together and form a bond, they settle into a lower-energy arrangement, and energy is released to the surroundings. Making a bond always releases energy.
Think of two strong magnets stuck together. Pulling them apart takes effort: that's bond breaking. Letting them snap together gives energy out: that's bond making. Neither magnet contains energy that pops out when you separate them.
So a reaction's overall energy change is a balance. Energy goes in to break the bonds in the reactants, and energy comes out when the bonds in the products form. If more energy comes out than went in, the reaction is exothermic overall.

## [07:59] E02S06 Enthalpy, delta H and talking about signs

Chemists summarise all of this with a quantity called enthalpy, written H. You can think of it as the total chemical energy, or heat content, of a system at constant pressure. We can't measure absolute enthalpy, but we can measure changes in it.
The enthalpy change, delta H, equals the enthalpy of the products minus the enthalpy of the reactants. On a diagram, reactants sit at one level and products at another. For the hydrogen chloride reaction, the products sit one hundred and eighty-three kilojoules lower.
Because the products are lower, delta H is negative. The system has less enthalpy at the end, and the difference was transferred out to the surroundings as heat. A negative delta H means exothermic. A positive delta H means the products sit higher, and the reaction is endothermic.
Now the language. Delta H equals negative one hundred and eighty-three kilojoules is a signed quantity describing the system. The same fact, said as energy released, is one hundred and eighty-three kilojoules released, a positive amount. Avoid saying "negative one hundred and eighty-three kilojoules released". That's a double negative, which would actually mean energy was absorbed.
Checkpoint. One student writes "delta H equals negative fifty kilojoules". Another writes "fifty kilojoules of energy is released". Are they describing the same reaction? Pause and decide.  *(pause)*
Yes. Both describe an exothermic change in which fifty kilojoules of energy leaves the system. One uses a signed enthalpy change, and the other uses the positive size of the energy released. Both are correct if each one is labelled clearly.

## [11:38] E02S08 Oxygen counts too: partly oxidised fuels

Combustion has one more bond to remember: oxygen's. When methane burns, the ledger breaks four carbon to hydrogen bonds and two oxygen to oxygen double bonds. Then it forms two carbon to oxygen double bonds in carbon dioxide and four oxygen to hydrogen bonds in water. Leaving out the oxygen bonds is a common slip.
Combustion releases so much energy largely because the bonds in carbon dioxide and water are very strong, so forming them releases a lot of energy.
Now compare three carbon compounds on a ladder of oxidation. Methane's carbon has no oxygen attached. In methanol, one oxygen is already attached to the carbon. Carbon dioxide is fully oxidised and can't burn any further. In oxidation numbers, the carbon goes from negative four, to negative two, to positive four.
A fuel that is already partly oxidised has, in a sense, already done some of its combustion. So burning it has less further oxidation to do, and that tends to release less energy. The data fit this idea: one mole of methane releases eight hundred and ninety kilojoules, while one mole of methanol, also with one carbon, releases seven hundred and twenty-six.
But treat this as a qualitative idea, not a shortcut. An exact comparison needs the full balanced reaction, the states of the products, and a consistent basis, such as per mole or per gram. We'll compare fuels carefully in later episodes.
