## E12S02 | Per mole, per gram, per useful output
objective: Show a ranking that changes with the basis (methane vs octane).
onscreen: Data card (CH4 -890 kJ/mol, M 16.0; C8H18 -5470 kJ/mol, M 114.0); bars per mole and per gram; third basis "per useful MJ" introduced.
transitions: Per-mole bars, then per-gram bars with the ranking flip.
checks: 890/16.0 = 55.6 kJ/g; 5470/114.0 = 48.0 kJ/g.

[b01] Let's compare methane and octane. Burning one mole of octane releases about five thousand four hundred and seventy kilojoules. One mole of methane releases eight hundred and ninety. Per mole, octane wins by a long way, because each octane molecule is much bigger.
[b02] Now per gram. Methane: eight hundred and ninety divided by sixteen point zero is about fifty-five point six kilojoules per gram. Octane: five thousand four hundred and seventy divided by one hundred and fourteen point zero is about forty-eight point zero. Per gram, methane wins.
[b03] Neither statement is wrong. They just answer different questions. And for a real heating task, there's a third basis that often matters most. How much fuel, and how much emission, does it take to deliver a given amount of useful energy? That depends on efficiency too.

## E12S03 | Per litre: another basis for gases
objective: Compare hydrogen and methane per gram and per litre at SLC; the ranking flips again.
onscreen: Data card (H2 -286 kJ/mol, M 2.0; CH4 -890 kJ/mol, M 16.0); per-gram bars (143 vs 55.6 kJ/g); per-litre bars at SLC (11.5 vs 35.9 kJ/L); "state the basis".
transitions: Per-gram bars, then per-litre bars with the ranking flip.
checks: 286/2.0 = 143 kJ/g; 890/16.0 = 55.6; 286/24.8 = 11.5 kJ/L; 890/24.8 = 35.9 kJ/L; ratio 3.1.

[b01 pause=8] For gaseous fuels, there's another basis that matters: energy per litre. Hydrogen releases two hundred and eighty-six kilojoules per mole, and its molar mass is only two point zero grams per mole. Methane releases eight hundred and ninety kilojoules per mole, and its molar mass is sixteen point zero. Which fuel is better per gram? And which is better per litre of gas at S L C? Pause and work it out.
[b02] Per gram, hydrogen wins easily. Two hundred and eighty-six, divided by its molar mass of two point zero, is one hundred and forty-three kilojoules per gram. Methane, from earlier, was about fifty-five point six.
[b03] But at S L C, a mole of any gas occupies twenty-four point eight litres. So to get the energy per litre, divide each energy per mole by that molar volume. For hydrogen, two hundred and eighty-six, divided by twenty-four point eight, is about eleven point five kilojoules per litre. For methane, eight hundred and ninety, divided by twenty-four point eight, is about thirty-five point nine. So per litre, methane wins by about three times. That's why storing enough hydrogen for a vehicle is a challenge, even though it's excellent per gram.

## E12S04 | Why oxygen-containing fuels release less energy
objective: Compare ethanol and propane per mole and per gram; explain the difference by partial oxidation (the hydroxy group), not by quoting bond enthalpies.
onscreen: Data (C2H5OH -1367 kJ/mol, M 46.0; C3H8 -2220 kJ/mol, M 44.0); per-gram bars 29.7 vs 50.5 kJ/g; condensed formulas with the O of ethanol highlighted; "already partly oxidised"; explanation card.
transitions: Data, then bars, then the highlighted oxygen.
checks: 1367/46.0 = 29.7 kJ/g; 2220/44.0 = 50.5 kJ/g.

[b01] Here's a comparison that shows an important idea. Ethanol and propane have almost the same molar mass: forty-six point zero and forty-four point zero grams per mole. But propane releases two thousand two hundred and twenty kilojoules per mole, and ethanol only one thousand three hundred and sixty-seven. Per gram, that's about fifty point five kilojoules for propane and twenty-nine point seven for ethanol.
[b02] So why are they so different? Ethanol already contains an oxygen atom, in its hydroxy group. That means part of the molecule is already oxidised. So there's less left to oxidise when it burns, and less energy is released.
[b03] That's the explanation to give. It isn't enough to say only that the two fuels have different bond enthalpies. A bond-enthalpy argument would need full calculations for both combustion equations. The same idea explains why methanol releases less energy per gram than ethanol. A bigger fraction of its mass is oxygen.

## E12S05 | Normalise to the same useful output
objective: Build the chain useful output -> input -> mass and emissions.
onscreen: Flow: 1.00 MJ useful <- divide by efficiency -> fuel energy input -> divide by energy per kg -> mass; x emission factor -> emissions; units on every arrow.
transitions: Chain builds right to left from the useful output.
checks: units: MJ / (MJ/kg) = kg; MJ x (g/MJ) = g.

[b01] So here's the fair way to compare. First, fix the task. Let's say it's to deliver one point zero zero megajoule of useful heat. Then work backwards for each fuel.
[b02] First, the fuel energy input is the useful output, divided by the efficiency. Next, the mass of fuel is that input, divided by the fuel's energy per kilogram. Finally, if emissions are given per megajoule of fuel energy, the emissions are that input, times the emission factor.
[b03] Watch the units. Megajoules divided by megajoules per kilogram gives kilograms. Megajoules times grams per megajoule gives grams. Now both fuels are being compared on exactly the same task.

## E12S08 | Drawing the system boundary
objective: Distinguish operational from lifecycle emissions by widening the boundary.
onscreen: Inner boundary around combustion; widened boundary adding feedstock growing, fertiliser, harvesting, processing (heat/electricity), transport, distribution; methane leakage arrow; labels operational vs lifecycle.
transitions: Boundary expands outward stage by stage.
checks: qualitative.

[b01] Where you draw the boundary changes the answer. If you draw a tight boundary around just the burning, you count operational, or direct, emissions. That's the carbon dioxide from combustion.
[b02] Now widen the boundary. Growing the feedstock may use fertiliser and machinery. Processing needs heat or electricity. And the fuel has to be transported and distributed. All of these can produce emissions. Together with combustion, they make up the lifecycle emissions.
[b03] Methane deserves a special mention. Biogas and natural gas systems can leak unburnt methane, from digesters, pipes or wells. And methane is a much more potent greenhouse gas than carbon dioxide. So even small leaks can outweigh other benefits. A claim about emissions should always say which boundary it uses.

## E12S09 | Is a biofuel carbon neutral?
objective: Biogenic carbon cycle vs net lifecycle emissions; "lower-carbon", not automatically "carbon neutral".
onscreen: Short cycle: CO2 absorbed by the growing crop -> biofuel -> CO2 released on combustion; extra arrows for fossil inputs (fertiliser, processing energy, transport) and methane leakage; verdict card.
transitions: Cycle arrows run once; extra arrows add on; verdict.
checks: qualitative.

[b01 pause=8] Time for another checkpoint. A company says its biofuel is carbon neutral, because the carbon dioxide released when it burns was absorbed by the crop as it grew. Is that claim fully justified? Pause and decide.
[b02] Part of the claim is right. The carbon in a biofuel was recently taken from the atmosphere by photosynthesis. So burning it returns carbon that was already in the short-term carbon cycle. That's unlike fossil carbon, which had been locked away for millions of years.
[b03] The whole lifecycle can add emissions. Fertiliser production, farm machinery, processing heat and transport can use fossil energy, and some systems leak methane. A biofuel might have lower lifecycle emissions than a fossil fuel, but we cannot claim it is lower carbon or carbon neutral without evidence comparing the full lifecycles.

## E12S10 | Renewable is not the same as sustainable
objective: Trade-offs (land, food, water, biodiversity); circular use of waste; green chemistry principles; SDGs.
onscreen: Trade-off cards; circular loop animation (food waste -> digester -> biogas heat; digestate -> fertiliser -> crops -> food -> waste); green chemistry principle chips; SDG chips.
transitions: Cards, then the loop runs once, then principle chips attach to parts of the loop.
checks: green chemistry principles named: renewable feedstocks, waste prevention, design for energy efficiency, catalysis.

[b01] A renewable feedstock regrows, but growing it still has costs. A fuel crop can compete with food crops for land. It can use large amounts of fresh water. And it can replace natural habitats. So a renewable fuel isn't automatically a sustainable one.
[b02] Using wastes avoids some of these trade-offs. Here's a circular example. Food waste goes into a digester, which produces biogas for heat. The leftover digestate becomes fertiliser for crops. The crops become food, and the food waste goes back to the start. So materials stay in use, instead of becoming waste.
[b03] This connects to several green chemistry principles. One is using renewable feedstocks, such as plant material or waste. Another is preventing waste, by turning by-products into inputs. There's also designing for energy efficiency, and using catalysts, like the enzymes in fermentation, so processes can run under milder conditions. And finally, there's aiming for a high atom economy, so more of the reactant atoms end up in the useful product, rather than in by-products.
[b04] It links to the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals, too: affordable and clean energy, responsible consumption and production, and climate action. And there's zero hunger, which is where the food-versus-fuel trade-offs matter.

## E12S11 | Practice Q24: build an evidence-based answer
objective: Less scaffolded Q24 built one sentence at a time with an honest data limit.
onscreen: Q24 prompt; comparison table A vs B; sentences appear one by one, each tagged with its evidence; limit card; conditional recommendation; marks.
transitions: Prompt, pause, then sentence-by-sentence build.
checks: B waste feedstock + CO2 use; A less listed energy (8 vs 10 MJ) and water (12 vs 20 L); emissions comparison not possible without emission factors.

[b01 pause=15] Here's practice question twenty-four. It's worth six marks. Pause the video and plan your answer before we build one together.
[b02] The first consideration is feedstock. Process B uses food-processing waste, while Process A uses an edible crop. So B avoids competing with food for land, and it makes productive use of a waste. That fits the green chemistry principle of using renewable feedstocks, and the circular-economy idea of keeping materials in use.
[b03] The second consideration is resource use. Per kilogram of ethanol, A uses less of the listed energy: eight megajoules, compared with ten for B. It also uses less fresh water: twelve litres, compared with twenty. So on those measures, A is better.
[b04] So there's a trade-off. B is better on feedstock, and on using its carbon dioxide. A is better on energy and water. But notice that capturing carbon dioxide and using it in another process isn't the same as storing it permanently. It may be released later.
[b05] Can we say which process has lower total lifecycle emissions? No. A uses natural-gas heat, and B uses electricity that's only sixty percent renewable. But we aren't given emission factors for either one, or any data on transport and feedstock production. Stating that limit clearly is worth a mark.
[b06] Finally, your recommendation must follow from stated priorities. For example, if avoiding competition with food is the priority, B is preferred, despite its higher energy and water use. Either process can be defended, as long as the reasoning matches the evidence. That's six marks: two plus two for the two supported comparisons, one for the trade-off or limit, and one for a consistent, conditional conclusion.
