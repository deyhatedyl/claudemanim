## E12S02 | Per mole, per gram, per useful output
objective: Show a ranking that changes with the basis (methane vs octane).
onscreen: Data card (CH4 -890 kJ/mol, M 16.0; C8H18 -5460 kJ/mol, M 114.0); bars per mole and per gram; third basis "per useful MJ" introduced.
transitions: Per-mole bars, then per-gram bars with the ranking flip.
checks: 890/16.0 = 55.6 kJ/g; 5460/114.0 = 47.9 kJ/g.

[b01] Let's compare methane and octane. Burning one mole of octane releases about five thousand four hundred and sixty kilojoules. One mole of methane releases eight hundred and ninety. Per mole, octane wins by a long way, because each octane molecule is much bigger.
[b02] Now per gram. Methane: eight hundred and ninety divided by sixteen point zero is about fifty-five point six kilojoules per gram. Octane: five thousand four hundred and sixty divided by one hundred and fourteen point zero is about forty-seven point nine. Per gram, methane wins.
[b03] Neither statement is wrong. They answer different questions. And for a real heating task there's a third basis that often matters most: how much fuel, and how much emission, does it take to deliver a given amount of useful energy? That depends on efficiency too.

## E12S03 | Per litre: another basis for gases
objective: Compare hydrogen and methane per gram and per litre at SLC; the ranking flips again.
onscreen: Data card (H2 -286 kJ/mol, M 2.0; CH4 -890 kJ/mol, M 16.0); per-gram bars (143 vs 55.6 kJ/g); per-litre bars at SLC (11.5 vs 35.9 kJ/L); "state the basis".
transitions: Per-gram bars, then per-litre bars with the ranking flip.
checks: 286/2.0 = 143 kJ/g; 890/16.0 = 55.6; 286/24.8 = 11.5 kJ/L; 890/24.8 = 35.9 kJ/L; ratio 3.1.

[b01 pause=8] Here's another basis that matters for gaseous fuels: energy per litre. Hydrogen releases two hundred and eighty-six kilojoules per mole, and its molar mass is only two point zero grams per mole. Methane releases eight hundred and ninety, with a molar mass of sixteen point zero. Which is better per gram, and which per litre of gas at SLC? Pause and work it out.
[b02] Per gram, hydrogen wins easily: two hundred and eighty-six divided by two point zero is one hundred and forty-three kilojoules per gram, compared with about fifty-five point six for methane.
[b03] But a mole of any gas at SLC occupies twenty-four point eight litres. So per litre, hydrogen gives two hundred and eighty-six divided by twenty-four point eight, about eleven point five kilojoules, while methane gives eight hundred and ninety divided by twenty-four point eight, about thirty-five point nine. Per litre, methane wins by about three times. That's why storing enough hydrogen for a vehicle is a challenge, even though it's excellent per gram.

## E12S04 | Normalise to the same useful output
objective: Build the chain useful output -> input -> mass and emissions.
onscreen: Flow: 1.00 MJ useful <- divide by efficiency -> fuel energy input -> divide by energy per kg -> mass; x emission factor -> emissions; units on every arrow.
transitions: Chain builds right to left from the useful output.
checks: units: MJ / (MJ/kg) = kg; MJ x (g/MJ) = g.

[b01] Here's the fair way to compare. Fix the task first: deliver one point zero zero megajoule of useful heat. Then, for each fuel, work backwards.
[b02] Fuel energy input equals useful output divided by efficiency. Mass of fuel equals that input divided by the fuel's energy per kilogram. And if emissions are given per megajoule of fuel energy, then emissions equal the input times that emission factor.
[b03] Watch the units: megajoules divided by megajoules per kilogram gives kilograms. Megajoules times grams per megajoule gives grams. Now both fuels are compared on exactly the same task.

objective: Show that a lower emission factor per input MJ can still give higher emissions per useful MJ.

But it's burned in a heater that's only twenty percent efficient. Which fuel has lower emissions per megajoule of useful heat? Pause and work it out.

## E12S07 | Drawing the system boundary
objective: Distinguish operational from lifecycle emissions by widening the boundary.
onscreen: Inner boundary around combustion; widened boundary adding feedstock growing, fertiliser, harvesting, processing (heat/electricity), transport, distribution; methane leakage arrow; labels operational vs lifecycle.
transitions: Boundary expands outward stage by stage.
checks: qualitative.

[b01] Where you draw the boundary changes the answer. Draw a tight boundary around just the burning, and you count operational, or direct, emissions: the carbon dioxide from combustion.
[b02] Now widen it. Growing the feedstock may use fertiliser and machinery. Processing needs heat or electricity. The fuel must be transported and distributed. All of these can produce emissions, and together with combustion, they make up the lifecycle emissions.
[b03] Methane deserves a special mention. Biogas and natural gas systems can leak unburnt methane, from digesters, pipes or wells. Methane is a much more potent greenhouse gas than carbon dioxide, so even small leaks can outweigh other benefits. A claim about emissions should always say which boundary it uses.

## E12S08 | Is a biofuel carbon neutral?
objective: Biogenic carbon cycle vs net lifecycle emissions; "lower-carbon", not automatically "carbon neutral".
onscreen: Short cycle: CO2 absorbed by the growing crop -> biofuel -> CO2 released on combustion; extra arrows for fossil inputs (fertiliser, processing energy, transport) and methane leakage; verdict card.
transitions: Cycle arrows run once; extra arrows add on; verdict.
checks: qualitative.

[b01 pause=8] Checkpoint. A company says its biofuel is carbon neutral, because the carbon dioxide released when it burns was absorbed by the crop as it grew. Is that claim fully justified? Pause and decide.
[b02] Part of it is right. The carbon in a biofuel was recently taken from the atmosphere by photosynthesis, so burning it returns carbon that was already in the short-term carbon cycle, unlike fossil carbon that had been locked away for millions of years.
[b03] But the whole lifecycle usually adds more. Fertiliser production, farm machinery, processing heat and transport often use fossil energy, and some systems leak methane. So a biofuel can have lower net emissions than a fossil fuel, but "carbon neutral" needs lifecycle evidence. Without it, "lower-carbon" is the most you can claim.

## E12S09 | Renewable is not the same as sustainable
objective: Trade-offs (land, food, water, biodiversity); circular use of waste; green chemistry principles; SDGs.
onscreen: Trade-off cards; circular loop animation (food waste -> digester -> biogas heat; digestate -> fertiliser -> crops -> food -> waste); green chemistry principle chips; SDG chips.
transitions: Cards, then the loop runs once, then principle chips attach to parts of the loop.
checks: green chemistry principles named: renewable feedstocks, waste prevention, design for energy efficiency, catalysis.

[b01] A renewable feedstock regrows, but growing it still has costs. A fuel crop can compete with food crops for land, use large amounts of fresh water, and replace natural habitats. So a renewable fuel isn't automatically a sustainable one.
[b02] Using wastes avoids some of these trade-offs. Here's a circular example: food waste goes into a digester, which produces biogas for heat; the leftover digestate becomes fertiliser for crops; the crops become food, and food waste returns to the start. Materials stay in use instead of becoming waste.
[b03] This connects to green chemistry principles. Using renewable feedstocks, such as plant material or waste. Preventing waste, by turning by-products into inputs. Designing for energy efficiency. And using catalysts, like the enzymes in fermentation, to make processes run under milder conditions.
[b04] It also links to the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals: affordable and clean energy, responsible consumption and production, climate action, and zero hunger, which is where food-versus-fuel trade-offs matter.

## E12S10 | Practice Q24: build an evidence-based answer
objective: Less scaffolded Q24 built one sentence at a time with an honest data limit.
onscreen: Q24 prompt; comparison table A vs B; sentences appear one by one, each tagged with its evidence; limit card; conditional recommendation; marks.
transitions: Prompt, pause, then sentence-by-sentence build.
checks: B waste feedstock + CO2 use; A less listed energy (8 vs 10 MJ) and water (12 vs 20 L); emissions comparison not possible without emission factors.

[b01 pause=15] Practice question Q twenty-four is worth six marks. Pause the video and plan your answer before we build one together.
[b02] First consideration: feedstock. Process B uses food-processing waste, while Process A uses an edible crop. So B avoids competing with food for land and makes productive use of a waste, which fits the green chemistry principle of using renewable feedstocks and the circular-economy idea of keeping materials in use.
[b03] Second consideration: resource use. Per kilogram of ethanol, A uses less listed energy, eight megajoules compared with ten, and less fresh water, twelve litres compared with twenty. On those measures, A is better.
[b04] So there's a trade-off: B is better on feedstock and on using its carbon dioxide; A is better on energy and water. Also note that capturing carbon dioxide and using it in another process isn't the same as storing it permanently; it may be released later.
[b05] Can we say which has lower total lifecycle emissions? No. A uses natural-gas heat, and B uses electricity that's only sixty percent renewable, and we aren't given emission factors for either, or data on transport and feedstock production. Stating that limit honestly is worth a mark.
[b06] A recommendation must follow from stated priorities. For example: if avoiding competition with food is the priority, B is preferred, despite its higher energy and water use. Either process can be defended, as long as the reasoning matches the evidence. Marks: two plus two for the two supported comparisons, one for the trade-off or limit, and one for a consistent, conditional conclusion.
