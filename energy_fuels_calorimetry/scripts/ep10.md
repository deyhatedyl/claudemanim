## E10S04 | From calorimeter heat to reaction enthalpy
objective: q(cal) = CF x deltaT; q(reaction) = -q(cal); molar enthalpy = q(reaction) / n with an explicit basis.
onscreen: Two columns: calorimeter (+) and reaction (-); arrow from reaction to calorimeter; deltaH = -q/n with "per mole of water formed" label.
transitions: Heat moves from the negative reaction column to the positive calorimeter column.
checks: sign convention only.

[b01] The calorimeter measures the heat it gained. So q for the calorimeter equals the calibration factor, times the temperature rise. For an exothermic reaction, that's a positive number.
[b02] That heat came from the reacting system. So, if we assume all the heat stays in the calorimeter, q for the reaction is the negative of q for the calorimeter. It's the same size, with the opposite sign.
[b03] To get a molar enthalpy, divide by the amount the enthalpy refers to. If we want delta H per mole of water formed, we divide by the moles of water formed. That's the amount set by the limiting reagent. It's not the amount of whichever reagent happens to be mentioned first.

transitions: Coefficients slide down next to the division.

[b01] Now let's look at an equation with a coefficient of two. Two sodium hydroxide plus sulfuric acid forms sodium sulfate plus two water. Delta H is negative one hundred and fourteen kilojoules, for the equation as written.
[b02] "As written" means one mole of this reaction. That's two moles of sodium hydroxide reacting with one mole of sulfuric acid, forming two moles of water. So one hundred and fourteen kilojoules is released per two moles of sodium hydroxide, or per two moles of water.
[b03] The amount of reaction that actually happens is called the reaction extent. It's the smallest value of amount divided by coefficient. Multiply the extent by one hundred and fourteen kilojoules to get the heat released. If you multiply the moles of sodium hydroxide directly by one hundred and fourteen, you'd double count it.
