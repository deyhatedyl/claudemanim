## E01S02 | One container, several different labels
objective: See mass, amount and volume as different measurements of the same sample.
onscreen: A flask with three labelled tags (mass in g, amount in mol, volume in mL); a "dozen" counting analogy; a second flask with a different substance.
transitions: Tags attach one at a time; the substance swap changes the amount tag but not the mass tag.
checks: 46.0 g ethanol = 1.00 mol; 46.0 g water = 2.56 mol (46.0/18.0 = 2.555...).

[b01] Picture a flask of liquid ethanol. We can describe the same sample in several ways. Its mass, in grams, is what a balance reads. Its volume, in millilitres or litres, is how much space it takes up. And its amount, in moles, is a count of how many particles it contains.
[b02] A mole is a counting unit, like a dozen, but enormous: about six point zero two times ten to the power of twenty-three particles. Chemists count in moles because reactions happen particle by particle, so the recipe of a reaction is written in particles, and therefore in moles.
[b03] Molar mass links mass to amount. It is the mass of one mole of a substance, in grams per mole. For ethanol it's forty-six point zero grams per mole, so forty-six grams of ethanol is exactly one mole.
[b04] Now let's swap the ethanol for the same mass of water: forty-six grams again. The mass label is unchanged. But water molecules are lighter, at eighteen grams per mole, so the same forty-six grams contains about two point five six moles. Same mass, different amount.
[b05] So these labels are not interchangeable. You cannot replace grams with moles, or millilitres with litres, without a relationship that connects them. The next scene gives you those relationships.

## E01S03 | Three ways to get moles, and why the units cancel
objective: Use n = m/M, n = cV and n = V/Vm with explicit unit cancellation; know when Vm = 24.8 applies.
onscreen: Three equation cards; units shown beside each symbol and struck out as they cancel; SLC conditions box; 250 mL checkpoint.
transitions: Each card builds relationship, then units, then cancellation.
checks: 0.250 L x 0.100 mol/L = 0.0250 mol; substituting 250 gives 25.0 (1000x too big).

[b01] First, from a mass. Amount in moles equals mass divided by molar mass. Watch the units. Mass is in grams. Molar mass is in grams per mole. Dividing grams by grams per mole cancels the grams and leaves moles. The units tell you the relationship is set up correctly.
[b02] Second, from a solution. Amount equals concentration times volume. Concentration is in moles per litre, so the volume must be in litres. Then litres cancel, and you're left with moles.
[b03] Third, from a gas. At standard laboratory conditions, which in this course means twenty-five degrees Celsius and one hundred kilopascals, one mole of any gas occupies about twenty-four point eight litres. This is the molar volume. Amount equals gas volume divided by molar volume: litres divided by litres per mole gives moles.
[b04] Two warnings about that last one. It applies only to gases, and only at those stated conditions. A hot exhaust gas at six hundred degrees takes up far more space per mole, so twenty-four point eight would give the wrong answer there.
[b05 pause=8] Now a checkpoint. A solution has a concentration of zero point one zero zero moles per litre, and you have two hundred and fifty millilitres of it. Why can't you multiply zero point one zero zero by two hundred and fifty? Pause and explain it in your own words.
[b06] Because the concentration is per litre, the volume must be in litres for the units to cancel. Two hundred and fifty millilitres is zero point two five zero litres. Multiplying gives zero point zero two five zero moles. If you multiply by two hundred and fifty instead, you get twenty-five, which is a thousand times too big. The units left over, millilitres times moles per litre, don't simplify to moles. That mismatch is your warning sign.

## E01S04 | The unit ladder
objective: Convert volume, mass, energy and time units with explicit factors and a direction check.
onscreen: A vertical ladder m3 -> L -> mL with x1000 steps; parallel ladders for g, J and time; a direction rule.
transitions: Values climb or descend the ladder; factor labels appear on each rung.
checks: 0.0850 m3 = 85.0 L = 85 000 mL; 72 000 J = 72.0 kJ = 0.0720 MJ; 0.150 h = 9.00 min = 540 s.

[b01] Unit conversions cause more lost marks than difficult chemistry, so let's make them mechanical. Here is a volume ladder. One cubic metre is one thousand litres, and one litre is one thousand millilitres. Each step down the ladder multiplies by a thousand.
[b02] Here's a direction rule. When you move to a smaller unit, you need more of them, so the number gets bigger. When you move to a larger unit, the number gets smaller. For example, zero point zero eight five zero cubic metres becomes eighty-five point zero litres, and then eighty-five thousand millilitres.
[b03] Mass works the same way. One kilogram is one thousand grams, and one gram is one thousand milligrams. Energy too: one megajoule is one thousand kilojoules, and one kilojoule is one thousand joules. So seventy-two thousand joules is seventy-two point zero kilojoules.
[b04] Time is the odd one out, because its steps are sixty, not a thousand. One hour is sixty minutes, and one minute is sixty seconds, so one hour is three thousand six hundred seconds. Zero point one five zero hours is nine minutes, which is five hundred and forty seconds.
[b05] After every conversion, ask one question: should the number have got bigger or smaller? If it went the wrong way, you multiplied when you should have divided.

## E01S05 | A balanced equation is a recipe in moles
objective: Read coefficients as particle ratios, then mole ratios; show they are not mass ratios.
onscreen: Particle groups for 2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O (schematic); batches scaled to moles; ratio line; mass check table.
transitions: Molecules group into batches; counts are replaced by amounts in mol.
checks: 0.40 mol H2 : 0.20 mol O2 : 0.40 mol H2O; masses 0.80 g + 6.4 g = 7.2 g.

[b01] A balanced equation is a recipe. Hydrogen burning in oxygen: two hydrogen molecules react with one oxygen molecule to make two water molecules. Count the atoms on each side. Four hydrogen atoms and two oxygen atoms before, and the same after. Nothing appears or disappears; the atoms are just rearranged.
[b02] Now scale the recipe up. Make it ten times bigger, or a million times bigger, and the ratio stays two to one to two. Scale it up by a mole, and the coefficients become amounts: two moles of hydrogen react with one mole of oxygen to make two moles of water.
[b03] So a coefficient gives a mole ratio. If zero point four zero moles of hydrogen reacts, it needs half as much oxygen, zero point two zero moles, and it makes zero point four zero moles of water. The ratio is valid because it comes from counting particles, and moles are a count.
[b04] But the coefficients are not a mass ratio. Zero point four zero moles of hydrogen has a mass of only zero point eight grams, while zero point two zero moles of oxygen has a mass of six point four grams. Mass is still conserved: zero point eight plus six point four gives seven point two grams of water. But the masses are not in a two to one to two ratio. Use the equation with moles, then convert to grams at the end.

## E01S06 | Round at the end, not along the way
objective: Keep unrounded intermediate values and round the reported answer to suitable significant figures.
onscreen: Two calculation paths for 17.765 L / 24.8 L/mol: early rounding versus carried precision.
transitions: The early-rounded path drifts; a calculator-memory icon carries the full value.
checks: 17.765/24.8 = 0.716330...; 17.8/24.8 = 0.717742... -> 0.718.

[b01] One last tool before the questions: rounding. Suppose an oxygen volume works out to seventeen point seven six five litres, and you need the amount in moles. If you round that volume to seventeen point eight first, then divide by twenty-four point eight, you get zero point seven one eight moles.
[b02] If you keep the full value instead, you get zero point seven one six three, which rounds to zero point seven one six moles. The early rounding changed the third significant figure. In a longer calculation, those small errors build up.
[b03] So keep unrounded values in your calculator, write the intermediate result with a few extra digits, and round only the final answer. Base the number of significant figures on the data you were given, which here is three.

## E01S07 | Practice Q01: two routes to an amount
objective: Guided solution of Q01 with given, asked, model, relationship and plausibility.

[b01 pause=12] Here's practice question Q zero one, worth four marks. Read it, then pause the video and try it before I work through it.
[b02] Let's read it together. We're given a mass of ethanol and its molar mass, and a gas volume at standard laboratory conditions. We're asked for two amounts in moles, and then an explanation. Each part needs a different relationship.
[b03] Before we calculate, what should the ethanol answer look like? One mole of ethanol is forty-six grams, and we have only zero point three four five grams, much less than a mole. So expect an answer far below one, roughly one hundredth of a mole.
[b04] Amount equals mass divided by molar mass: zero point three four five grams divided by forty-six point zero grams per mole. The grams cancel, leaving zero point zero zero seven five zero moles. That's under one hundredth of a mole, which fits the prediction.
[b05] For the gas, the volume is in millilitres, but the molar volume is in litres per mole. So convert first. One hundred and eighty-six millilitres is zero point one eight six litres. Then divide by twenty-four point eight litres per mole. Litres cancel, giving zero point zero zero seven five zero moles.
[b06] So both samples contain the same amount. Does that mean the same mass? No. Equal amounts mean equal numbers of particles. The mass also depends on molar mass, and we don't know which gas this is, so we can't calculate its mass. Saying "we can't tell" here is a correct chemical answer, not a gap.
[b06b] Compare that with a weaker answer: "No, because they are different substances." It sounds reasonable, but it misses the causal link. Different substances matter here because their molar masses can differ, and mass equals amount times molar mass.
[b07] Here are the indicative marks. One mark for the ethanol amount, two for converting the gas volume and dividing by the molar volume, and one for explaining that equal moles don't imply equal masses.
[b08] Now the tempting error. Dividing one hundred and eighty-six by twenty-four point eight gives seven point five zero moles. The first wrong step is substituting millilitres where litres are needed. A sanity check catches it: seven and a half moles of gas would fill about one hundred and eighty-six litres, not one hundred and eighty-six millilitres.
[b09 pause=6] Quick prediction. If the gas had been measured at fifty degrees Celsius instead, could you still divide by twenty-four point eight? Think about it.
[b10] No. Twenty-four point eight litres per mole applies only at twenty-five degrees and one hundred kilopascals. At a higher temperature, a mole of gas occupies more volume, so you would need the conditions-specific molar volume.
