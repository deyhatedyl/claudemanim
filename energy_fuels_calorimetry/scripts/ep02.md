## E02S02 | System, surroundings, heat and temperature
objective: Draw the system boundary; define heat as energy transfer; separate heat from temperature.
onscreen: Calorimeter cup with water (surroundings, blue) and a dashed boundary around the reacting chemicals (system, orange); energy-transfer arrow across the boundary; bath vs cup comparison.
transitions: Boundary drawn first, then labels, then the transfer arrow.
checks: none numerical.

[b01] To talk about energy changes precisely, we split the world in two. The system is the reacting chemicals themselves, the substances whose bonds are changing. Everything else is the surroundings: the water they're dissolved in, the cup, the thermometer, the air.
[b02] When a reaction happens, energy can be transferred across the boundary between the system and the surroundings. Energy transferred because of a temperature difference is called heat. So heat is not a substance that flows around. It's a name for energy on the move.
[b03] Temperature is different. Temperature measures how hot something is, which reflects the average kinetic energy of its particles. It is not the total amount of energy something contains.
[b04] Here's an example. A cup of tea at eighty degrees is hotter than a bath at forty degrees. But the bath contains far more water, so it holds far more thermal energy. Higher temperature does not mean more energy. In calorimetry we'll use temperature changes to work out how much heat was transferred.

## E02S03 | Exothermic and endothermic
objective: Classify reactions by direction of energy transfer; give q(system) and q(surroundings) opposite signs.
onscreen: Two panels with the same system/surroundings picture; arrows out (exothermic) and in (endothermic); thermometers rising/falling; sign table.
transitions: Left panel builds, then right panel mirrors it.
checks: Ideal energy balance: q(system) = -q(surroundings).

[b01] In an exothermic reaction, energy is transferred from the system to the surroundings. The surroundings gain that energy, so the water warms up and the thermometer reading rises. Combustion of a fuel is exothermic.
[b02] In an endothermic reaction, energy is transferred from the surroundings into the system. The surroundings lose energy, so the water cools and the thermometer reading falls. An instant cold pack is a familiar example.
[b03] We can attach signs to these transfers. Energy gained counts as positive, and energy lost counts as negative. In an exothermic reaction the system loses energy, so q for the system is negative, while q for the surroundings is positive.
[b04] If no energy escapes anywhere else, these two are equal in size and opposite in sign. Whatever the system loses, the surroundings gain. That single idea, energy is conserved and only moves, underpins every calorimetry calculation in this series.

## E02S04 | Breaking bonds absorbs energy; making bonds releases it
objective: Show energy input when a bond breaks and energy output when a bond forms.
onscreen: Schematic H2 molecule; energy arrow in, atoms separate; atoms approach, energy arrow out; magnet analogy; label "schematic".
transitions: Break sequence, then make sequence, then side-by-side summary.
checks: Never show breaking as release.

[b01] So where does the energy come from? Start with a single hydrogen molecule. The two atoms are held together by a bond, an attraction between the nuclei and the shared electrons. To pull the atoms apart, you have to supply energy. Breaking a bond always absorbs energy.
[b02] Now run it the other way. When two separate hydrogen atoms come together and form a bond, they settle into a lower-energy arrangement, and energy is released to the surroundings. Making a bond always releases energy.
[b03] Think of two strong magnets stuck together. Pulling them apart takes effort: that's bond breaking. Letting them snap together gives energy out: that's bond making. Neither magnet contains energy that pops out when you separate them.
[b04] So a reaction's overall energy change is a balance. Energy goes in to break the bonds in the reactants, and energy comes out when the bonds in the products form. If more energy comes out than went in, the reaction is exothermic overall.

[b01 pause=12] Practice question Q zero three is worth four marks. Read it, then pause and try it.
[b02] We're asked for the enthalpy change for the equation as written, so for one mole of hydrogen reacting with one mole of chlorine to form two moles of hydrogen chloride. The model is: energy absorbed to break all reactant bonds, minus energy released when all product bonds form.
[b03] Start the ledger with bonds broken. One hydrogen to hydrogen bond, at four hundred and thirty-six kilojoules per mole, and one chlorine to chlorine bond, at two hundred and forty-three. Total energy absorbed: six hundred and seventy-nine kilojoules.
[b04] Now bonds formed. The equation makes two moles of hydrogen chloride, so two hydrogen to chlorine bonds form, each releasing four hundred and thirty-one. Total energy released: eight hundred and sixty-two kilojoules. Don't miss that coefficient of two.
[b05] Before subtracting, predict the sign. More energy is released than absorbed, so the reaction should be exothermic, with a negative enthalpy change.
[b06] Enthalpy change equals energy absorbed minus energy released: six hundred and seventy-nine minus eight hundred and sixty-two, which is negative one hundred and eighty-three kilojoules for the equation as written. It's an estimate, because average bond enthalpies are averages over many compounds.
[b07] For the explanation mark, link cause and effect: the reaction is exothermic because more energy is released forming the two hydrogen to chlorine bonds than is absorbed breaking the hydrogen to hydrogen and chlorine to chlorine bonds. Simply saying "bonds are formed" misses the comparison, which is the whole point.
[b08] Marks: one for the broken total, one for the formed total, one for the subtraction with the correct sign, and one for the causal explanation.
[b09] Two tempting errors. Adding every bond energy together gives a large positive number with no physical meaning. And treating bond breaking as the release step flips the sign, giving positive one hundred and eighty-three. In both cases, the first wrong step is not separating energy in from energy out.
[b10 pause=6] Changed condition. Suppose the product bond were weaker, so that forming the two new bonds released only six hundred kilojoules in total. Would the reaction still be exothermic?
[b11] No. Six hundred and seventy-nine absorbed minus six hundred released gives positive seventy-nine kilojoules, so it would be endothermic. The sign depends on the balance between breaking and forming, not on bonds being broken or formed alone.

## E02S06 | Enthalpy, delta H and talking about signs
objective: Define deltaH = H(products) - H(reactants) on an enthalpy level diagram; separate signed deltaH from released magnitude.
onscreen: Enthalpy axis with reactant and product levels; downward deltaH arrow; two statement cards (signed vs magnitude); incorrect wording crossed out.
transitions: Levels drawn first, arrow second, then the language panel.
checks: deltaH = -183 kJ <=> 183 kJ released; never "-183 kJ released".

[b01] Chemists summarise all of this with a quantity called enthalpy, written H. You can think of it as the total chemical energy, or heat content, of a system at constant pressure. We can't measure absolute enthalpy, but we can measure changes in it.
[b02] The enthalpy change, delta H, equals the enthalpy of the products minus the enthalpy of the reactants. On a diagram, reactants sit at one level and products at another. For the hydrogen chloride reaction, the products sit one hundred and eighty-three kilojoules lower.
[b03] Because the products are lower, delta H is negative. The system has less enthalpy at the end, and the difference was transferred out to the surroundings as heat. A negative delta H means exothermic. A positive delta H means the products sit higher, and the reaction is endothermic.
[b04] Now the language. Delta H equals negative one hundred and eighty-three kilojoules is a signed quantity describing the system. The same fact, said as energy released, is one hundred and eighty-three kilojoules released, a positive amount. Avoid saying "negative one hundred and eighty-three kilojoules released". That's a double negative, which would actually mean energy was absorbed.
[b05 pause=8] Checkpoint. One student writes "delta H equals negative fifty kilojoules". Another writes "fifty kilojoules of energy is released". Are they describing the same reaction? Pause and decide.
[b06] Yes. Both describe an exothermic change in which fifty kilojoules of energy leaves the system. One uses a signed enthalpy change, and the other uses the positive size of the energy released. Both are correct if each one is labelled clearly.

## E02S07 | Practice Q04: an endothermic change

[b01 pause=12] Practice question Q zero four is worth three marks. Pause the video and answer all three parts.
[b02] The dissolution absorbs energy, so energy is transferred from the calorimeter into the dissolving system. Every arrow points inward this time.
[b03] The system's energy change is the energy absorbed per mole times the amount: eighteen point zero kilojoules per mole times zero point zero two five zero moles, which is positive zero point four five zero kilojoules. It's positive because the system gains energy.
[b04] The calorimeter supplies that energy, so its change is negative zero point four five zero kilojoules. And because the calorimeter's contents lose energy, the temperature falls.
[b05] One mark each: the system's change with its positive sign, the calorimeter's change with its negative sign, and the falling temperature. The tempting error is giving both the same sign. Ask yourself who gains and who loses, and the signs follow.

## E02S08 | Oxygen counts too: partly oxidised fuels
objective: Include O=O bonds in combustion ledgers; place CH4, CH3OH and CO2 on a qualitative oxidation ladder with an explicit limit.
onscreen: Methane combustion bond ledger (counts only); oxidation ladder CH4 -> CH3OH -> CO2 with oxidation numbers; data card (-890 vs -726 kJ/mol); caution box about basis.
transitions: Ledger, then ladder, then data and caution.
checks: Ox. numbers of C: CH4 -4, CH3OH -2, CO2 +4. deltaH_c: CH4 -890, CH3OH -726 kJ/mol (liquid water).

[b01] Combustion has one more bond to remember: oxygen's. When methane burns, the ledger breaks four carbon to hydrogen bonds and two oxygen to oxygen double bonds. Then it forms two carbon to oxygen double bonds in carbon dioxide and four oxygen to hydrogen bonds in water. Leaving out the oxygen bonds is a common slip.
[b02] Combustion releases so much energy largely because the bonds in carbon dioxide and water are very strong, so forming them releases a lot of energy.
[b03] Now compare three carbon compounds on a ladder of oxidation. Methane's carbon has no oxygen attached. In methanol, one oxygen is already attached to the carbon. Carbon dioxide is fully oxidised and can't burn any further. In oxidation numbers, the carbon goes from negative four, to negative two, to positive four.
[b04] A fuel that is already partly oxidised has, in a sense, already done some of its combustion. So burning it has less further oxidation to do, and that tends to release less energy. The data fit this idea: one mole of methane releases eight hundred and ninety kilojoules, while one mole of methanol, also with one carbon, releases seven hundred and twenty-six.
[b05] But treat this as a qualitative idea, not a shortcut. An exact comparison needs the full balanced reaction, the states of the products, and a consistent basis, such as per mole or per gram. We'll compare fuels carefully in later episodes.

[b01] Let's recap. The system is the reacting chemicals; the surroundings are everything else. Heat is energy transferred, and temperature measures how hot something is. Breaking bonds absorbs energy, and making bonds releases it. Delta H is products minus reactants, and a negative value means energy was released to the surroundings.
