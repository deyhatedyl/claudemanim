## E03S02 | The axes and the two endpoints
objective: Set up an energy profile: enthalpy axis, reaction-coordinate axis (not time), reactant and product levels first.
onscreen: Axes labelled "Enthalpy" and "Reaction coordinate"; crossed-out "time"; reactant level then product level; deltaH arrow.
transitions: Axes draw, the x-axis label is emphasised, levels appear before any curve.
checks: Endothermic and exothermic examples use products below/above reactants.

[b01] An energy profile has enthalpy on the vertical axis. The horizontal axis is called the reaction coordinate, or reaction progress. It is not time. It shows how far the atoms have got in rearranging from reactants into products, so a profile says nothing about how fast a reaction goes.
[b02] Start every profile with the two endpoints. Draw a flat level for the reactants on the left, and a flat level for the products on the right. For an exothermic reaction, the products sit lower.
[b03] The vertical gap between the two levels is delta H. Measured from reactants down to products, it's negative here. Notice that delta H only depends on where the two endpoints sit. We haven't drawn anything in between yet, and we don't need to in order to know delta H.

## E03S03 | Activation energy, forward and reverse
objective: Add the curve; measure forward Ea, reverse Ea and deltaH as vertical distances.
onscreen: Smooth profile through the transition state; three labelled arrows (forward Ea, reverse Ea, deltaH); brief endothermic profile.
transitions: Curve grows from reactants; arrows appear one at a time.
checks: forward Ea = TS - R; reverse Ea = TS - P; deltaH = P - R; Ea(reverse) = Ea(forward) - deltaH.

[b01] Now join the endpoints with a curve. To get from reactants to products, bonds must start breaking before new ones fully form, so the energy first rises to a maximum, called the transition state, and then falls to the products.
[b02] The activation energy is the minimum energy needed to reach the transition state from the reactants. On the graph, it's the vertical distance from the reactant level up to the peak. That's the forward activation energy.
[b03] Run the reaction backwards, from products to reactants, and you climb from the product level up to the same peak. That's the reverse activation energy. For an exothermic reaction it's bigger than the forward one, because the products start lower.
[b04] So there are three vertical distances, and every one of them is a difference between two heights. Forward activation energy: peak minus reactants. Reverse activation energy: peak minus products. Delta H: products minus reactants. None of them is read straight off the axis.
[b05] For an endothermic reaction the picture flips. The products sit above the reactants, so delta H is positive, and the forward activation energy is the larger of the two.

## E03S04 | What a catalyst changes, and what it doesn't
objective: Overlay a catalysed path with identical endpoints; deltaH and heat released unchanged.
onscreen: Original profile; a lower dashed catalysed curve; Ea arrows for both; endpoints highlighted as unchanged; checkpoint.
transitions: Catalysed curve draws over the original; arrows compare.
checks: Same R and P levels; deltaH identical; both Ea values lowered by the same amount.

[b01] A catalyst provides an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy. On the profile, that's a second curve with a lower peak. Let's draw it as a dashed line over the original.
[b02] Look at the endpoints. The catalysed path starts at the same reactant level and finishes at the same product level. The catalyst is not consumed, and it doesn't change what the reactants and products are, so their enthalpies don't change.
[b03] So the catalyst lowers the forward activation energy, and the reverse activation energy by the same amount. But delta H is exactly the same.
[b04 pause=8] Checkpoint. A student says: "Adding a catalyst makes an exothermic reaction release more heat." Is that right? Pause and decide, with a reason.
[b05] No. For the same amount reacting, the heat released equals the size of delta H, and the catalyst doesn't change delta H. The reaction may reach the same result faster, but the energy released for that amount is unchanged.

## E03S06 | Thermochemical equations: scale and reverse
objective: Double an equation and its deltaH together; reverse an equation and change the sign; state the amount deltaH refers to.
onscreen: 2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O(l), deltaH = -572 kJ; doubled version; reversed version; halved per-mole statement.
transitions: Coefficients and deltaH scale together; reversal flips arrow and sign.
checks: x2: -1144 kJ; reversed: +572 kJ; per mol H2 or per mol H2O: -286 kJ mol^-1.

[b01] A thermochemical equation is a balanced equation with a delta H attached. Here's hydrogen burning to form liquid water: delta H equals negative five hundred and seventy-two kilojoules. That value refers to the equation as written: two moles of hydrogen reacting with one mole of oxygen to form two moles of liquid water.
[b02] If you double the equation, you double the amounts reacting, so you double delta H. Four moles of hydrogen burning releases twice as much energy: delta H becomes negative one thousand one hundred and forty-four kilojoules. The coefficients and delta H always scale together.
[b03] If you reverse the equation, the same energy change runs the other way, so the sign flips. Decomposing two moles of liquid water into hydrogen and oxygen has delta H equal to positive five hundred and seventy-two kilojoules. It takes as much energy to pull water apart as was released when it formed.
[b04] Sometimes you want a molar quantity for one substance. Dividing the equation by two gives one mole of hydrogen forming one mole of water, with delta H equal to negative two hundred and eighty-six kilojoules. So the molar enthalpy of combustion of hydrogen is negative two hundred and eighty-six kilojoules per mole of hydrogen. Always say which substance "per mole" refers to.

## E03S07 | States matter: liquid or gaseous water
objective: Show on a level diagram why forming H2O(g) releases less energy than forming H2O(l).
onscreen: Three levels: 2H2 + O2, 2H2O(g), 2H2O(l); vaporisation gap; deltaH values -572 and about -484 kJ; state labels highlighted.
transitions: Liquid-water level first; gas level inserted above it; arrows compare.
checks: deltaH(vap) about 44 kJ per mol at 25 C; -572 + 2 x 44 = -484 kJ.

[b01] Look closely at the states in that equation. The water is a liquid. What if the product were water vapour instead?
[b02] Turning liquid water into vapour absorbs energy: about forty-four kilojoules per mole at twenty-five degrees. So gaseous water sits higher in enthalpy than the same amount of liquid water.
[b03] That means forming two moles of water vapour releases less energy than forming two moles of liquid water: about four hundred and eighty-four kilojoules instead of five hundred and seventy-two. You can't reuse negative five hundred and seventy-two if the product state changes. You need data for the states actually involved.
[b04] This is why states are never decoration in a thermochemical equation. Hot exhaust gases contain water vapour, while a calorimeter at room temperature mostly ends up with liquid water. Which value applies depends on the states.

## E03S09 | Recap and closing recall
objective: Consolidate profile reading and equation manipulation.
onscreen: Summary profile with three arrows; rules for scaling/reversing; recall question; next episode.
transitions: Summary builds; recall with timer.
checks: none

[b01] To recap. On an energy profile, every quantity is a vertical difference: forward activation energy, reverse activation energy and delta H. A catalyst lowers the peak but leaves both endpoints, and delta H, unchanged. In a thermochemical equation, delta H scales with the coefficients and changes sign when the equation is reversed, and it depends on the states.
[b02 pause=10] Closing recall. An equation has delta H equal to negative one hundred kilojoules. What is delta H for the reverse equation with every coefficient tripled?
[b03] Reverse it: positive one hundred. Triple it: positive three hundred kilojoules. Next episode, we step back from equations to the fuels themselves: fossil fuels, biofuels and the carbon cycle.
