## E11S03 | Why the highest reading is too low
objective: Explain that cooling begins before the peak is reached, so the observed maximum underestimates the rise.
onscreen: Same graph; schematic arrows showing heat leaving during the rise; observed maximum marked as "measured".
transitions: Arrows appear along the rising section.
checks: none numerical.

[b01] Here's the problem. The calorimeter starts losing heat as soon as it's warmer than the room. And that happens while the reaction is still going. So by the time the temperature peaks, some heat has already escaped.
[b02] That means the highest measured temperature is too low. It's lower than the calorimeter would have reached if the reaction had been instantaneous, with no heat escaping. If you use that peak, you'll underestimate the temperature rise. And so you'll underestimate the heat released, too.

## E11S06 | Drawing the extrapolation well
objective: Hand-drawing skills: choose the steady region, balance the line, extend it to the mixing time, state the limits.
onscreen: Hypothetical scattered readings; poor line that includes rising points; best-fit line through the steady cooling region only; vertical guide at the mixing time; both extrapolated values marked; checklist.
transitions: Points appear; poor line, then best line; checklist ticks.
checks: both lines fitted with np.polyfit from the plotted points; data labelled hypothetical.

[b01] In an exam, you'll often draw this line by hand, and real readings are rarely perfectly straight. Here's a hypothetical set of readings, with a little scatter. So which points should your line follow?
[b02] Use only the steady cooling region. Leave out the points recorded while the temperature was still rising, because the reaction was still releasing heat then. If those points drag your line towards them, it gets the wrong slope, and it extrapolates to the wrong value. And don't force the line through the highest point just because it's the highest.
[b03] Draw one straight line that balances the points, with roughly as many above it as below. Then, with a ruler, extend it to a vertical line at the moment of mixing. Read the temperature where the two lines meet, and report it to a precision your graph can support.
[b04] If the cooling region curves, a straight-line extrapolation is less reliable. In that case, use the part of the trend that's closest to the mixing time, and say that the corrected value is an estimate.

## E11S07 | When the temperature falls
objective: Endothermic temperature-time graph: extrapolate the warming line back to mixing to find the corrected minimum.

[b01 pause=8] The same idea works for an endothermic process, where the temperature falls. Here's a hypothetical example. First, the baseline is flat at twenty-two point zero degrees. Mixing happens at sixty seconds, and the temperature drops to its lowest reading, eighteen point nine degrees. Then it creeps back up. Why does it rise again? Pause and think.
[b02] Once the mixture is colder than the room, heat flows in from the surroundings, so the mixture warms steadily. But that warming began while the process was still absorbing heat. So the lowest reading isn't as low as it would have been without any heat gain.
[b03] So extrapolate the warming line back to the mixing time. The line rises by zero point two degrees every sixty seconds. So going back sixty seconds from the lowest reading gives eighteen point seven degrees. The corrected change is eighteen point seven minus twenty-two point zero, which is negative three point three degrees. Using the lowest reading instead gives negative three point one.
[b04] The temperature change is negative, so q for the calorimeter is negative, and delta H for the process is positive. If you don't correct for the heat gained, the size of that fall comes out too small, and so does the size of delta H.

## E11S08 | Heat loss during calibration versus during the reaction
objective: Separate the two heat-loss situations and follow each through its formula.
onscreen: Two panels: calibration (CF = E/deltaT, deltaT down -> CF up) and reaction with a valid CF (q = CF x deltaT, deltaT down -> |q| down -> |deltaH| down); arrows on each symbol.
transitions: Left panel, then right panel, with up/down arrows.
checks: directions as in the required table rows 1-2.

[b01] Now, heat can escape at two different stages, and the effects go in different directions. That's why the rule "heat loss makes the answer smaller" isn't reliable.
[b02] During calibration, the electrical energy is known correctly. But heat loss makes the measured temperature rise smaller. The calibration factor is E over delta T, so dividing by a smaller rise makes it come out larger.
[b03] Now the reaction, with a valid calibration factor already fixed. Uncorrected heat loss makes the measured rise smaller. Then q, which is the calibration factor times delta T, is smaller. So the calculated heat released is smaller, and so is the size of the molar enthalpy.
[b04] So it's the same physical cause, but with opposite effects on the calculated quantity. That's because the temperature rise sits in the denominator of one formula, and in the numerator of the other. Always follow the measurement through the actual formula.

## E11S09 | Systematic and random errors; accuracy and precision
objective: Define systematic vs random errors, accuracy, precision, repeatability and validity; show tight grouping around a biased value.
onscreen: Number line with a true value; repeated results tightly grouped but offset (precise, inaccurate); scattered results around the true value (imprecise, accurate on average); definition cards.
transitions: Dots appear one at a time; labels follow.
checks: definitions consistent with VCE science skills usage.

[b01] Now some vocabulary, and we'll use it precisely. A random error makes repeated measurements scatter unpredictably, sometimes high and sometimes low. A systematic error pushes every result in the same direction, by a consistent amount or proportion.
[b02] Accuracy is how close a result is to the true value. Precision describes how closely repeated results agree with each other. Here's a set of results that are tightly grouped, so they're precise. But they're all too low, so they're not accurate. That's the fingerprint of a systematic error.
[b03] Repeatability means that when the experiment is repeated by the same person, with the same method and equipment, the results agree closely. Good repeatability shows precision, but it doesn't prove accuracy. Validity asks whether the experiment actually measures what it claims to measure, under suitable controlled conditions.
[b04] Averaging more repeats reduces the effect of random errors. But it does nothing about a systematic error. You just get a better estimate of a biased value.

## E11S10 | Resolution, mistakes and outliers
objective: Define resolution (with a unit) for scales and digital displays; resolution is not accuracy; separate mistakes from errors; outliers.
onscreen: Thermometer graduated every 1 degC, digital probe reading to 0.1 degC, digital balance to 0.01 g, each with its resolution and unit; "higher resolution: finer increments, not automatically more accurate"; mistake card; outlier card.
transitions: Instruments appear; resolution chips attach; cards replace them.
checks: qualitative (resolution = smallest graduation or last displayed digit, quoted with a unit).

[b01 pause=8] Here's one more measurement idea that exams test directly: resolution. Which has the higher resolution: a thermometer marked every one degree, or a digital probe that reads to zero point one of a degree? Pause and decide.
[b02] Resolution is the smallest change an instrument can show, and it's always stated with a unit. On a scale, it's the smallest graduation, so it's one degree for that thermometer. On a digital display, it's the last digit it can show. That's zero point one degree for the probe, or zero point zero one gram for a balance that reads to two decimal places. So the probe has the higher resolution, because it records finer increments.
[b03] But higher resolution isn't the same as accuracy. A probe that reads to zero point one of a degree can still be badly calibrated. What resolution does limit is how precisely you can quote a temperature change. That's why a rise read from a coarse thermometer supports fewer significant figures.
[b04] And here are two last terms. A mistake, like misreading a scale or spilling some solution, isn't an error in the scientific sense. Identify it, leave that result out, and repeat the measurement. And a single outlier, in an otherwise precise set, isn't evidence of a systematic error. Investigate it, and report how you treated it.
