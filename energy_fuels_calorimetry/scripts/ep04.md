## E04S02 | What counts as a fuel: the fossil fuels
objective: Define a fuel; describe coal, natural gas and petrol; explain why fossil fuels are non-renewable.
onscreen: Definition card; three fossil-fuel cards (main components); geological formation strip.
transitions: Definition, then cards one by one, then the timescale strip.
checks: coal mostly carbon; natural gas mostly methane; petrol a mixture of hydrocarbons (e.g. octane, C8H18).

[b01] A fuel is a substance that undergoes a chemical reaction, usually combustion with oxygen, to release useful energy, most often as heat. The useful part matters: we choose fuels because the energy can be captured and used.
[b02] The fossil fuels are coal, natural gas and petrol. Coal is mostly carbon. Natural gas is mostly methane. Petrol is a mixture of liquid hydrocarbons refined from crude oil, such as octane.
[b03] Fossil fuels formed from the remains of organisms buried and transformed by heat and pressure over millions of years. We're using them far faster than they can form, so on any human timescale they are non-renewable.

## E04S03 | Renewable means replenished on a human timescale
objective: Contrast geological and short feedstock timescales; introduce biogas, bioethanol and biodiesel.
onscreen: Two timelines (millions of years vs months to years); three biofuel cards with feedstock and process; "same molecule, different origin" note.
transitions: Timelines scale in; biofuel cards appear under the short cycle.
checks: biogas mainly CH4 + CO2 from anaerobic digestion; bioethanol by fermentation; biodiesel by transesterification.

[b01] Compare two timescales. A fossil fuel took millions of years to form. A biofuel is made from biomass, such as crops, plant waste or animal fat, that can be regrown or replaced within months or years. A fuel is renewable if it is replenished at least as fast as we use it.
[b02] There are three biofuels to know. Biogas is a mixture of mainly methane and carbon dioxide, made when microbes break down organic waste without oxygen. Bioethanol is ethanol made by fermenting sugars. And biodiesel is made by reacting plant oils or animal fats with an alcohol, in a process called transesterification.
[b03] Notice something important. Methane from biogas and methane from natural gas are the same molecule. Burning one mole of either releases the same energy and forms the same carbon dioxide. What differs is where the carbon came from, and how quickly it can be replaced.

## E04S04 | Following the carbon atoms
objective: Track labelled carbon atoms around a biofuel carbon cycle; contrast with fossil carbon.
onscreen: Cycle: atmosphere CO2 -> plant (photosynthesis, light in) -> fuel (fermentation) -> combustion -> atmosphere; labelled C atoms move around the loop; fossil branch adds stored carbon.
transitions: Atoms travel stage by stage; the fossil branch appears last.
checks: carbon atoms conserved at every stage.

[b01] Let's follow some carbon atoms. They start in carbon dioxide in the atmosphere. During photosynthesis, a plant uses energy from sunlight to build those carbon atoms into glucose.
[b02] The glucose can be fermented into ethanol. The same carbon atoms are now in the fuel, with some leaving as carbon dioxide during fermentation.
[b03] When the ethanol burns, its carbon atoms return to the atmosphere as carbon dioxide, and the next crop can take them up again. Over a full cycle, this carbon is recycled on a timescale of months or years.
[b04] Burning a fossil fuel is different. Its carbon was locked away underground for millions of years. Releasing it adds extra carbon dioxide to the atmosphere, faster than natural processes can remove it.
[b05] But careful: the biofuel cycle isn't automatically carbon neutral. Growing, harvesting, processing and transporting the fuel can use fossil energy too. We'll look at those wider boundaries in episode twelve.

## E04S05 | Photosynthesis and respiration
objective: Balanced equations; light as the energy source for photosynthesis; respiration as the reverse energy change.
onscreen: Photosynthesis equation with a light arrow into the system; respiration equation with energy out; enthalpy level sketch; atom counts.
transitions: Photosynthesis first, then respiration mirrors it.
checks: 6CO2 + 6H2O -> C6H12O6 + 6O2 balanced; respiration reverse; about 2.8 x 10^3 kJ per mol glucose.

[b01] Photosynthesis: six carbon dioxide plus six water forms one glucose plus six oxygen. Check the atoms: six carbons, twelve hydrogens, and eighteen oxygens on each side.
[b02] Photosynthesis is endothermic. The products, glucose and oxygen, sit higher in enthalpy than carbon dioxide and water. That energy doesn't appear from nowhere: it is absorbed from sunlight. Light energy is converted into chemical energy.
[b03] Cellular respiration is the reverse overall change. Glucose reacts with oxygen to form carbon dioxide and water, releasing roughly two point eight thousand kilojoules per mole of glucose. As we saw in episode two, that energy is released because the bonds formed in carbon dioxide and water are stronger, not because glucose's bonds break.

## E04S06 | Making bioethanol: fermentation and distillation
objective: Atom-conserving fermentation; enzyme conditions; distillation concentrates but need not purify.
onscreen: Glucose schematic splitting into 2 ethanol + 2 CO2 with atom counters; yeast/enzyme note with a temperature panel; simplified distillation diagram (schematic).
transitions: Atoms move without vanishing; conditions panel; distillation diagram builds left to right.
checks: C6H12O6 -> 2C2H5OH + 2CO2 balanced (24 atoms each side); ethanol bp 78 C vs water 100 C; distillate typically up to about 95% ethanol.

[b01] Fermentation: glucose in solution forms two ethanol and two carbon dioxide. Watch the atoms. Six carbons, twelve hydrogens and six oxygens go in, and exactly the same atoms come out, just rearranged.
[b02] This reaction is catalysed by enzymes from yeast, in the absence of oxygen, at a warm temperature, around thirty to thirty-five degrees. Enzymes are proteins with a specific shape. At a very high temperature, their shape is permanently damaged. We say the enzyme is denatured, and the fermentation slows or stops.
[b03] Fermentation only produces a dilute ethanol solution, around ten to fifteen percent ethanol, because the yeast can't survive higher concentrations. So we separate and concentrate the ethanol by distillation.
[b04] Distillation uses the difference in boiling points. Ethanol boils at about seventy-eight degrees, water at one hundred. When the mixture is heated, the vapour is richer in ethanol than the liquid, and it's cooled and collected in a condenser. The distillate is much more concentrated, but ordinary distillation can't produce pure ethanol: it reaches about ninety-five percent at most.

## E04S08 | Biogas and biodiesel
objective: Describe anaerobic digestion; show basic transesterification as an ester exchange (schematic).
onscreen: Digester schematic (waste in, biogas out: CH4 + CO2, digestate); triglyceride + 3 CH3OH -> 3 methyl esters + glycerol ester-exchange diagram with catalyst label.
transitions: Digester first; then the three fatty-acid chains move from glycerol to methanol.
checks: triglyceride + 3 alcohol -> 3 fatty-acid alkyl esters + glycerol; balanced example C57H110O6 + 3CH3OH -> 3C19H38O2 + C3H8O3.

[b01] Biogas comes from anaerobic digestion. Organic waste, like manure, food scraps or sewage, is sealed in a tank without oxygen. Microbes break it down over days to weeks, producing a gas that's mainly methane and carbon dioxide. The leftover solid, called digestate, can be used as fertiliser.
[b02] Biodiesel starts from fats and oils, which are triglycerides. A triglyceride is three fatty acid chains attached to a glycerol backbone by ester links.
[b03] In transesterification, the triglyceride reacts with three molecules of an alcohol, usually methanol, with a catalyst such as potassium hydroxide. Each fatty acid chain swaps partners: it leaves the glycerol and attaches to the alcohol. So one triglyceride plus three alcohol molecules gives three fatty acid methyl esters, which are the biodiesel, plus one glycerol.
[b04] For this series, that ratio is the key: one triglyceride, three alcohol, three esters, one glycerol. The detailed organic chemistry belongs to the organic chemistry topic; here we just need to recognise the reaction and why the product is a renewable fuel.

## E04S09 | Practice Q08: same molecule, different source
objective: Less scaffolded Q08; checkpoint that renewable is not emission-free or automatically sustainable.
onscreen: Q08 prompt; two methane sources side by side converging on the same CH4 molecule; answers; lifecycle issues; marks; trap.
transitions: Prompt, pause, side-by-side comparison.
checks: identical energy and CO2 per mol; renewability by timescale; leakage, processing energy or land use.

[b01 pause=15] Practice question Q zero eight is worth four marks. Pause the video and write your answers.
[b02] Part a. Both fuels are pure methane, so per mole burned under the same conditions they release the same energy and produce the same amount of carbon dioxide: one mole of carbon dioxide per mole of methane. The molecule doesn't remember where it came from.
[b03] Part b. Methane from natural gas is non-renewable: it formed over millions of years and is used far faster than it's replaced. Biomethane from recently grown plant waste is renewable, because its feedstock regrows on a human timescale.
[b04] Part c. Renewable doesn't prove sustainable. Methane can leak from digesters and pipelines, and methane is a much more potent greenhouse gas than carbon dioxide. Processing also uses energy, and growing feedstock uses land and water. Sustainability needs the whole lifecycle.
[b05] One mark for part a, two for part b, and one for part c. The trap is saying that biomethane doesn't produce carbon dioxide when it burns. It does, exactly as much per mole. The difference is that its carbon was recently taken from the atmosphere.
[b06] So, the checkpoint for this episode: renewable means replenished on a human timescale. It doesn't mean emission-free, and it doesn't automatically mean sustainable.
