[b01] Welcome to episode five. Your body runs on chemical energy too. Today we treat food as a fuel: where its energy comes from, and how to calculate and compare the energy in different foods.

## E05S02 | Respiration: food as a fuel
objective: Show respiration as oxidation of glucose; oxygen reduced 0 -> -2; energy conserved but not all becomes useful work.
onscreen: Respiration equation; oxidation numbers of O above O2 and in products; energy split into useful work and heat (schematic bar).
transitions: Equation, then oxidation numbers, then energy split.
checks: O: 0 in O2; -2 in CO2 and H2O.

[b01] In respiration, glucose reacts with oxygen to form carbon dioxide and water, just like the combustion of a fuel, but in many small, enzyme-controlled steps instead of a flame. The overall equation and the overall energy change are the same as burning glucose.
[b02] Look at the oxygen. In oxygen gas, its oxidation number is zero. In carbon dioxide and in water, it's negative two. A decrease in oxidation number means oxygen is reduced. At the same time, the glucose is oxidised. Respiration is a redox reaction.
[b03] Energy is conserved. The energy released doesn't disappear and isn't created. Some is captured to power muscles and cell processes, but a large share is released as heat, which keeps your body warm. So not all of the released energy becomes useful work.

## E05S03 | Energy factors and the energy bar
objective: Build total food energy from three macronutrient contributions using the supplied factors.
onscreen: Factor table (carbohydrate 16, protein 17, fat 37 kJ per g); worked example label per 100 g; stacked energy bar with three segments and the total.
transitions: Each segment is calculated, then added to the bar.
checks: 60 x 16 = 960; 8 x 17 = 136; 15 x 37 = 555; total 1651 kJ per 100 g.

[b01] Food labels give the mass of each macronutrient. To estimate energy, we use average energy factors: carbohydrate provides about sixteen kilojoules per gram, protein about seventeen, and fat about thirty-seven. Fat provides more than twice as much energy per gram as the other two.
[b02] Here's an example: a muesli bar label, per one hundred grams, lists sixty grams of carbohydrate, eight grams of protein and fifteen grams of fat. These are illustrative label values.
[b03] Each nutrient contributes its mass times its factor. Carbohydrate: sixty grams times sixteen kilojoules per gram is nine hundred and sixty kilojoules. Protein: eight times seventeen is one hundred and thirty-six. Fat: fifteen times thirty-seven is five hundred and fifty-five. The grams cancel each time, leaving kilojoules.
[b04] Add the three contributions: one thousand six hundred and fifty-one kilojoules per one hundred grams. Notice that fat, at only fifteen grams, supplies a third of the total. That's the effect of its larger factor.

## E05S04 | Per 100 g, per serving, per packet
objective: Scale a per-100 g value to a serving with units attached; separate total energy from energy per gram.
onscreen: Label value (kJ per 100 g); scaling fraction with units; serving result; energy per gram unchanged; checkpoint with a doubled serving.
transitions: Units cancel visibly in the scaling step.
checks: 1651 x 45/100 = 742.95 -> 743 kJ; 16.51 kJ per g; doubled serving 1486 kJ, same 16.5 kJ per g.

[b01] Labels report energy on different bases: per one hundred grams, per serving, and sometimes per packet. To convert, keep the units attached. A forty-five gram serving is forty-five hundredths of one hundred grams.
[b02] So the serving energy is one thousand six hundred and fifty-one kilojoules per one hundred grams, times forty-five grams. The grams cancel, and we divide by one hundred: about seven hundred and forty-three kilojoules per serving.
[b03] The energy per gram is the same whether you eat a crumb or a whole packet: about sixteen point five kilojoules per gram for this bar. Per gram is a property of the food. Per serving depends on how much you eat.
[b04 pause=6] Checkpoint. If you eat two servings instead of one, what happens to the energy you take in, and what happens to the energy per gram?
[b05] The energy you take in doubles, to about one thousand four hundred and eighty-six kilojoules. The energy per gram doesn't change: it's still about sixteen point five kilojoules per gram.

## E05S05 | Nutrition factors are a different basis
objective: Distinguish rounded nutritional factors from a measured molar heat of combustion.
onscreen: Two cards: nutrition factor (16 kJ per g, average metabolisable energy) vs glucose heat of combustion (about 2.8 x 10^3 kJ per mol, about 15.6 kJ per g); caution box.
transitions: Cards side by side; conversion shown on the right card.
checks: 2.8 x 10^3 / 180 = 15.6 kJ per g.

[b01] The sixteen kilojoules per gram for carbohydrate is a rounded nutritional factor. It's an average for the energy the body can obtain from carbohydrates in typical foods.
[b02] A measured heat of combustion is different. For pure glucose burned completely, it's about two point eight thousand kilojoules per mole. Dividing by the molar mass, one hundred and eighty grams per mole, gives about fifteen point six kilojoules per gram.
[b03] The two numbers are close, but they're answers to different questions, reported on different bases. In a question, use the data and the basis you're given. Don't quietly swap a food factor for a molar heat of combustion, or the other way around.

## E05S07 | Which food has more energy? Choose the basis first
objective: Show a ranking that flips between per-100 g and per-serving bases (illustrative values).
onscreen: Two foods: nuts (2500 kJ per 100 g, 25 g serving) and cooked pasta (650 kJ per 100 g, 250 g serving); two bar charts on the two bases; a "state your basis" prompt.
transitions: Per-100 g bars first, then per-serving bars with the ranking flip highlighted.
checks: nuts serving 625 kJ; pasta serving 1625 kJ.

[b01] Which food has more energy? That question isn't complete until you say: more energy per what? Here are two foods with illustrative label values. Nuts: two thousand five hundred kilojoules per one hundred grams. Cooked pasta: six hundred and fifty kilojoules per one hundred grams.
[b02] Per one hundred grams, the nuts win easily, because they're rich in fat and contain little water.
[b03] But a typical serving of nuts might be twenty-five grams, which is six hundred and twenty-five kilojoules. A serving of pasta might be two hundred and fifty grams, which is one thousand six hundred and twenty-five kilojoules. Per serving, the pasta wins.
[b04] Both statements are true. They answer different questions. In an exam, state the basis, show the numbers for both foods on that basis, and only then make the comparison.

## E05S09 | Recap and closing recall

[b01] To recap. Respiration oxidises food just as combustion oxidises a fuel, and the energy is conserved, partly as useful work and partly as heat. Food energy is estimated with factors of sixteen, seventeen and thirty-seven kilojoules per gram. Keep units attached when scaling, and always state the basis of a comparison.
