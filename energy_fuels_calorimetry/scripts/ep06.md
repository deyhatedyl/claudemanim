## E06S02 | Balancing complete combustion: C, then H, then O
objective: Balance an oxygenated fuel with conserved atom counters, remembering the oxygen already in the fuel.
onscreen: Methanol combustion skeleton; colour-coded C/H/O counters for each side; coefficients appear step by step; final doubled equation.
transitions: Counters update after every coefficient change.
checks: CH3OH + 1.5O2 -> CO2 + 2H2O; x2: 2CH3OH + 3O2 -> 2CO2 + 4H2O.

[b01] In complete combustion, a fuel burns in plenty of oxygen, and all its carbon ends up as carbon dioxide and all its hydrogen as water. Let's balance the complete combustion of methanol, a fuel that already contains an oxygen atom.
[b02] Balance carbon first. Methanol has one carbon, so we need one carbon dioxide. The counters now match for carbon.
[b03] Next, hydrogen. Methanol has four hydrogen atoms: three on the carbon and one on the oxygen. Each water has two, so we need two water molecules.
[b04] Oxygen last, because oxygen gas is the only reactant we can adjust freely. The products now contain two oxygens in carbon dioxide plus two in the waters: four in total. Methanol already supplies one of them. So oxygen gas must supply the other three, which is one and a half oxygen molecules.
[b05] A half is fine in a thermochemical equation, but to get whole numbers, double everything: two methanol plus three oxygen gives two carbon dioxide plus four water. Check the counters: two carbons, eight hydrogens, eight oxygens on each side.

## E06S03 | A hydrocarbon from petrol

Doubling gives two octane plus twenty-five oxygen forming sixteen carbon dioxide plus eighteen water.

## E06S04 | Incomplete combustion: products need to be specified
objective: Compare complete and specified incomplete product sets; incomplete products are not determined by "limited oxygen" alone.
onscreen: Three balanced methane equations side by side (CO2; CO; C); oxygen required per mol CH4 (2, 1.5, 1); note on CO toxicity and soot; warning card.
transitions: Columns appear one at a time; O2 demand bars compare.
checks: CH4 + 2O2 -> CO2 + 2H2O; 2CH4 + 3O2 -> 2CO + 4H2O; CH4 + O2 -> C + 2H2O.

[b01] When oxygen is limited, combustion can be incomplete. Some of the carbon ends up as carbon monoxide, a toxic gas, or as solid carbon, which we see as soot. The hydrogen still forms water.
[b02] Here are three balanced equations for methane. Complete combustion needs two moles of oxygen per mole of methane. Burning to carbon monoxide needs one and a half. Burning to soot needs only one. Less oxygen per mole of fuel, and less energy released, because the carbon is less fully oxidised.
[b03] But here's the subtle point. Saying "limited oxygen" doesn't tell you which of these happens. A real flame usually makes a mixture of carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide and soot, in proportions that depend on the conditions. So you can only write an incomplete-combustion equation when the products, or enough data about them, are specified.

[b04] Carbon next. Ethanol has two carbons, so x plus y equals two.

Coming out: two x in carbon dioxide, plus y in carbon monoxide, plus three in the water. So two x plus y plus three equals six, which means two x plus y equals three.
[b06] Subtract the carbon equation from that: x equals one. Then y equals one. So one mole of carbon dioxide, one mole of carbon monoxide and three moles of water vapour.
[b07] The balanced equation: ethanol plus two and a half oxygen forms carbon dioxide plus carbon monoxide plus three water vapour. Complete combustion would need three moles of oxygen per mole of ethanol, so this reaction had half a mole too little.

If you forget the oxygen inside ethanol, the oxygen balance becomes two x plus y equals two, and you'd conclude that all the carbon became carbon monoxide. That looks like the textbook answer for "limited oxygen", but it's wrong for this data.

## E06S06 | Hot exhaust and dry gas
objective: Track products from a hot exhaust to a cooled, dried, measured gas; separate "formed" from "measured".
onscreen: Exhaust stream schematic (hot: CO2, H2O(g), N2, excess O2); cooler with water condensing; dry gas to measurement; two accounting columns.
transitions: Particles flow left to right; water drops out in the cooler.
checks: water is a gas above 100 C at 100 kPa and mostly liquid at 25 C.

[b01] Now think about the gases themselves. In a hot exhaust, well above one hundred degrees, the water formed by combustion is a gas: water vapour. Alongside it are carbon dioxide, nitrogen from the air, and any excess oxygen.
[b02] If the exhaust is cooled to room temperature, most of the water vapour condenses to liquid water and can be removed. What remains is called dry gas. Carbon dioxide stays a gas, so it can now be collected and measured.
[b03] So keep two accounting columns. One lists what the reaction formed, including water vapour. The other lists what is actually measured after cooling and drying. A question about greenhouse gases in a hot exhaust includes the water vapour; a question about a dry gas measured at standard laboratory conditions doesn't.
[b04] And gas volumes always need their conditions. Twenty-four point eight litres per mole applies at twenty-five degrees and one hundred kilopascals. A hot gas at six hundred degrees occupies roughly three times as much volume per mole, so you can't apply the standard molar volume to the hot exhaust.

## E06S08 | Moles, not grams; and which greenhouse gases?
objective: Coefficient ratios are mole ratios; separate carbon emissions from all greenhouse gases in a stated model.
onscreen: CH4 combustion: 1 mol CO2 (44 g) vs 2 mol H2O (36 g) comparison bars; checkpoint card; greenhouse-gas boundary card (CO2, H2O vapour, CH4).
transitions: Mole bars, then mass bars that reverse the comparison.
checks: 1 x 44.0 = 44 g; 2 x 18.0 = 36 g.

[b01 pause=6] Checkpoint. Methane combustion forms twice as many moles of water as carbon dioxide. Does it form twice the mass of water? Pause and decide.
[b02] No. Per mole of methane: one mole of carbon dioxide is forty-four grams, and two moles of water is thirty-six grams. Twice the moles, but less mass, because a water molecule is much lighter. Coefficients give mole ratios. Masses also depend on the molar masses.
[b03] One more distinction. "Carbon emissions" usually means carbon dioxide, or the mass of carbon it contains. "Greenhouse gases" is broader: carbon dioxide, water vapour, unburnt methane, and others. So always check which gases a question's model counts before you add masses together.
