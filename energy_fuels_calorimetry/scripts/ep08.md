## E08S02 | Where the energy goes
objective: Energy-flow diagram for a spirit burner heating water; useful vs lost energy.
onscreen: Spirit burner under a metal can of water with a thermometer; labelled arrows: fuel energy -> water (useful, solid green), -> can (solid grey), -> air (dashed red), incomplete combustion (soot).
transitions: Diagram builds; arrows appear one at a time.
checks: none numerical.

[b01] Here's a common way to measure the energy from a fuel. A spirit burner holds the liquid fuel. Above it, a metal can holds a measured mass of water, with a thermometer to track its temperature.
[b02] When the fuel burns, the energy it releases goes several places. Some heats the water: that's the useful energy we can measure. Some heats the can and the thermometer. And a lot simply heats the surrounding air, especially the hot gases rising past the sides of the can.
[b03] There's another loss too. In a small flame, combustion is often incomplete. You may see soot on the bottom of the can. Incompletely burned fuel releases less energy than the equation for complete combustion predicts.
[b04] So the energy gained by the water is always less than the energy the fuel could release. Our job is to measure that useful part, and compare it with the total.

## E08S03 | Building q = mc delta T
objective: Derive q = mc deltaT from the meaning of specific heat capacity; identify whose m and deltaT.
onscreen: One gram of water warmed by one degree (4.18 J); scaling by mass and by temperature rise; final formula with units; "whose mass?" checklist.
transitions: Grid of gram blocks scales up; then the temperature scale stretches.
checks: 4.18 J g^-1 C^-1; 250 g x 4.18 x 17.2 = 17 974 J.

[b01] Start small. To warm one gram of water by one degree Celsius takes four point one eight joules. That number is the specific heat capacity of water, c: four point one eight joules per gram per degree.
[b02] Two grams need twice as much energy for the same one-degree rise, and two hundred and fifty grams need two hundred and fifty times as much. So energy is proportional to the mass of water.
[b03] And warming the same water by ten degrees instead of one takes ten times the energy. So energy is also proportional to the temperature rise.
[b04] Put those together: q equals m times c times delta T. Check the units: grams, times joules per gram per degree, times degrees. Grams cancel, degrees cancel, leaving joules.
[b05] Now the important questions. Whose mass? The water's, because the water is what's being heated, not the fuel. And delta T is the temperature rise, final minus initial, not the final temperature itself.

## E08S04 | Mass and energy change the temperature rise
objective: Show separately how water mass and energy input affect deltaT (rearranged deltaT = q / mc).
onscreen: Three panels with thermometers: 4180 J into 100 g (10 C); 4180 J into 200 g (5 C); 8360 J into 100 g (20 C).
transitions: Panels appear in sequence, temperature columns animate.
checks: 4180/(100 x 4.18) = 10; 4180/(200 x 4.18) = 5; 8360/(100 x 4.18) = 20.

[b01] Rearranging gives delta T equals q divided by m c. Let's see what that predicts. Put four thousand one hundred and eighty joules into one hundred grams of water: the temperature rises ten degrees.
[b02] Put the same energy into two hundred grams of water: the rise halves, to five degrees. The energy is spread over twice as much water.
[b03] Now double the energy, to eight thousand three hundred and sixty joules, into the original one hundred grams: the rise doubles, to twenty degrees. Keep these two effects in mind whenever a question changes the mass of water or the amount of fuel.

## E08S05 | How much fuel burned? A mass reading
objective: Find fuel burned from before/after mass readings; apply the same reasoning to food calorimetry with a different basis.
onscreen: Balance readings before and after; mass lost; n = m/M; food-calorimetry card (1.50 g food, 100.0 g water, +8.0 C); comparison with a nutrition label basis.
transitions: Balance digits change; subtraction; then food example builds.
checks: 3344 J; 3344/1.50 = 2229 J/g = 2.2 kJ/g.

[b01] To know how much fuel burned, weigh the burner before and after heating. The mass lost is the mass of fuel burned, assuming nothing else escaped. Divide by molar mass to get the amount in moles, then multiply by the molar enthalpy of combustion to get the energy the fuel released.
[b02] The same idea measures energy in food. Suppose one point five zero grams of dry food burns under a can holding one hundred point zero grams of water, and the water warms by eight point zero degrees. The water gains one hundred times four point one eight times eight point zero: three thousand three hundred and forty-four joules.
[b03] Per gram of food, that's three thousand three hundred and forty-four divided by one point five zero: about two point two kilojoules per gram. That's far less than a typical nutrition label value of fifteen or more kilojoules per gram for a dry, carbohydrate-rich food.
[b04] Two reasons. First, most of the energy escaped to the can and the air, and some food probably didn't burn completely. Second, a label reports the energy the body can obtain from food, which is a different basis from heat measured in a can. So call this result an experimental water-heat yield, and don't treat it as the food's label energy.

## E08S07 | Efficiency, forwards and backwards
objective: Define efficiency; rearrange to input = useful output / efficiency; test limiting cases.
onscreen: Efficiency fraction with labelled numerator/denominator; rearrangement animation; two bars for the same useful output at 100% and 45% efficiency; checkpoint.
transitions: Fraction terms move to show the rearrangement; bars compare inputs.
checks: 45.144 kJ at 100% -> 45.1 kJ input; at 45% -> 100.3 kJ.

[b01] Efficiency compares what you get with what you put in. Efficiency equals useful energy output divided by total energy input, times one hundred percent. Always say what counts as useful, and keep both energies in the same unit.
[b02] Sometimes you know the useful energy you need, and the efficiency, and you want the input. Rearrange: total input equals useful output divided by the efficiency, written as a fraction, like zero point four five zero.
[b03] Test it with limiting cases. At one hundred percent efficiency, the input would just equal the useful output. At forty-five percent, dividing by zero point four five zero makes the input more than twice as big. A less efficient heater needs more fuel to deliver the same useful energy. Dividing by a number less than one makes the answer larger, which is exactly what should happen.
[b04 pause=6] Checkpoint. A student finds the required input by multiplying the useful energy by the efficiency. Will their answer be too big or too small?
[b05] Too small. Multiplying by zero point four five zero gives less than the useful output itself, which would mean getting more energy out than you put in. That's impossible.

## E08S09 | Why measured values come out low
objective: Identify the main errors in a can-and-burner experiment, their direction, and specific improvements.
onscreen: Spirit burner under a can of water with labelled loss arrows (hot gases, can and clamp, sooty flame, fuel evaporating from the wick); direction chip "calculated |deltaH| too small"; improvement list.
transitions: Loss arrows appear one at a time; improvements replace them.
checks: qualitative; evaporation overstates the mass burned, so q / n is too small.

[b01 pause=8] Checkpoint. A class burns ethanol under a can of water and calculates an enthalpy of combustion much smaller in size than the data book value. List as many reasons as you can. Pause and think.
[b02] Most of the energy never reaches the water. Hot gases carry heat away around the can, the can and the clamp warm up, and draughts make it worse. A yellow, sooty flame means incomplete combustion, so less energy is released per gram than the data book assumes. And if ethanol evaporates from the wick before the burner is reweighed, the recorded mass loss includes fuel that never burned.
[b03] Each of these makes the calculated energy per mole, and the size of delta H, too small. The fixes are specific: use a draught shield and a lid, keep the flame close to a thin-walled copper can, stir the water, make sure there's enough air for a clean blue flame, and cap the burner and reweigh it straight away. With this method, a result well below the data book value is expected; a result above it would point to a measurement or calculation error instead.

## E08S10 | Recap and closing recall
objective: Consolidate q = mc deltaT, mass loss, and efficiency in both directions.
onscreen: Summary chain fuel -> energy -> water -> efficiency; recall; answer; next episode.
transitions: Chain builds; recall with timer.
checks: halving efficiency doubles the input and the fuel mass.

[b01] To recap. The fuel's energy comes from the mass burned, converted to moles, times the molar enthalpy of combustion. The useful energy comes from q equals m c delta T for the water. Efficiency is useful over total, and to find an input from a required output, divide by the efficiency.
[b02 pause=8] Closing recall. If a heater's efficiency halved, how would the mass of fuel needed for the same heating task change?
[b03] It would double, because the required input is the useful output divided by the efficiency. Next episode: why a real calorimeter heats more than just its water, and how calibration accounts for it.
