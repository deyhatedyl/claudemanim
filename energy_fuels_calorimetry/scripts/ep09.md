## E09S02 | More than the water gets heated
objective: Split energy input between the water and the apparatus (cup, probe, stirrer, heater).
onscreen: Insulated calorimeter with heater coil, stirrer and probe; energy input arrow splitting into "water" and "cup, probe, stirrer" arrows; definition card for CF.
transitions: Calorimeter builds; energy splits into two labelled streams.
checks: none numerical.

[b01] Here's a solution calorimeter. It's an insulated cup with a lid, holding a measured volume of water, with a stirrer, a temperature probe and an electric heater.
[b02] When energy goes in, it doesn't just warm the water. It also warms the inside of the cup, the probe, the stirrer and the heater itself. All of those heat up as well, so each one absorbs some of the energy too.
[b03] So instead of using m c delta T for the water alone, we use a single number for the whole setup. It's called the calibration factor, or C F for short. It's the energy needed to raise the temperature of the calorimeter and its contents by one degree Celsius, in joules per degree.
[b04] The calibration factor belongs to this particular calorimeter, with these particular contents. Change the cup, the probe, or the amount of liquid inside, and the calibration factor changes too.

## E09S03 | Two kinds of calorimeter
objective: Distinguish a solution calorimeter from a bomb calorimeter; both need a calibration factor.
onscreen: Side by side: solution calorimeter (insulated cup, lid, stirrer, probe) and schematic bomb calorimeter (sealed steel bomb with sample and excess oxygen, ignition wire, surrounding water, stirrer, thermometer, insulated jacket); labels; "both: q = CF x deltaT".
transitions: Solution calorimeter first, then the bomb calorimeter builds from the inside out.
checks: qualitative.

[b01] There are two main designs you'll meet. The first is a solution calorimeter: an insulated cup with a lid. It's used for reactions that happen in solution, like neutralisation or dissolving.
[b02] The second is a bomb calorimeter, which is used for combustion. The fuel or food sample sits inside a sealed steel container, called the bomb. It's filled with excess oxygen under pressure. A current through an ignition wire starts the combustion. The heat then passes through the steel into a measured amount of water around it. And all of that sits inside an insulated jacket.
[b03] Because the bomb is sealed and well insulated, combustion is complete, and very little heat escapes. So it gives much more accurate values than burning a fuel under a can of water. But in both designs, the water isn't the only thing that heats up. That's why both need a calibration factor.
[b04] The design also tells you which errors to look for. With an open cup, or a fuel burned under a can, the main problem is heat escaping to the surroundings. An open flame may also burn incompletely. A sealed, insulated bomb reduces both problems. That's why it's used to measure energy values for fuels and foods.

## E09S04 | Electrical calibration: E = VIt, then CF = E / delta T
objective: Derive and apply E = VIt with time in seconds; CF = E / deltaT.
onscreen: Heater circuit with voltmeter and ammeter; E = VIt with unit analysis (V x A x s = J); time conversion box; CF = E / deltaT with units.
transitions: Each quantity lights up on the circuit, then appears in the formula.
checks: 6.00 x 1.50 x 240 = 2160 J; 4 min = 240 s.

[b01] To find the calibration factor, we put in a known amount of energy, and measure the temperature rise. An electric heater is ideal for this, because we can measure its energy very precisely.
[b02] The electrical energy is voltage, times current, times time. In symbols, E equals V times I times t. Voltage is in volts, current in amps, and time in seconds. Volts times amps gives watts, which are joules per second. So multiplying by seconds leaves joules.
[b03] The time must be in seconds. So a four-minute heating period is two hundred and forty seconds. If you used four instead of two hundred and forty, the energy would come out sixty times too small.
[b04] Then the calibration factor is the energy supplied, divided by the temperature rise it caused. That's E over delta T, and its units are joules per degree Celsius.

## E09S05 | Stacking heat capacities and the lower bound
objective: Model CF as water heat capacity plus apparatus heat capacity; derive CF >= m(water) c(water).
onscreen: Stacked bar: water (m c) + apparatus (C_app) = CF; lower-bound marker at m c; inequality.
transitions: Water block, then apparatus block stacks on top.
checks: CF = mc + C_app; C_app > 0 => CF > mc.

[b01] It helps to think of the calibration factor as two parts stacked together. The water's part is its mass, times its specific heat capacity. The apparatus part is the energy per degree needed to warm the cup, probe, stirrer and heater.
[b02] Add them together, and you get the calibration factor. This simple model assumes the parts just add, and that their heat capacities don't change over the small temperature range we use.
[b03] Here's a useful check. The apparatus always needs some energy to warm up, so its part is positive. That means a correctly measured calibration factor must be larger than the water's part alone. In other words, the calibration factor is at least m c for the water.
[b04] So if a calculated calibration factor comes out smaller than the water alone could account for, something's gone wrong, either in the measurements or in the calculation.

[b02] No, we can't. The water's part grows. One hundred and fifty point zero grams, times four point one eight, is six hundred and twenty-seven point zero joules per degree. ## E09S08 | Calibrating with a reaction of known delta H
objective: Chemical calibration: q = n x |deltaH| for a reference reaction, CF = q / deltaT; compare with electrical calibration.
onscreen: Requirements chips; hypothetical reference reaction (50.0 kJ released per mol); 0.0400 mol -> 2.00 kJ = 2000 J; deltaT 3.70 degC -> CF = 541 J/degC; comparison cards: electrical vs chemical.
transitions: Lines write; comparison cards slide in.
checks: 0.0400 x 50.0 = 2.00 kJ; 2000 / 3.70 = 540.5 -> 541 J/degC.

[b01] There's also a second way to calibrate. Instead of a heater, you carry out a reaction whose enthalpy change is already known accurately. You use a known amount of it, in the same calorimeter, with the same contents.
[b02] Here's a hypothetical example. A reference reaction releases fifty point zero kilojoules per mole, and zero point zero four zero zero moles of it react. So it releases two point zero zero kilojoules, which is two thousand joules. Say the temperature rises by three point seven zero degrees. Then the calibration factor is two thousand, divided by three point seven zero. That's about five hundred and forty-one joules per degree.
[b03] Electrical calibration is usually preferred. That's because the energy is measured directly and precisely, from the voltage, current and time. A chemical calibration relies on two things: the reference reaction being complete, and its enthalpy value being accurate. Either way, calibrate under the same conditions as the experiment you'll use the factor for.

## E09S09 | Heat loss during calibration: follow the formula
objective: Predict the direction of the CF error when heat escapes during calibration.
onscreen: CF = E / deltaT with E fixed and an arrow down on deltaT; resulting arrow up on CF; warning against a memorised rule; checkpoint.
transitions: Arrows appear on each symbol in turn.
checks: E measured electrically (unchanged); deltaT smaller => CF larger.

[b01] Now suppose some heat escapes during the electrical calibration. Which way does that push the calibration factor? Don't guess. Follow the formula.
[b02] The electrical energy comes from the measured voltage, current and time, so it's still correct. It doesn't change. But some of that energy leaks out instead of warming the calorimeter. So the measured temperature rise is smaller than it should be.
[b03] The calibration factor is E divided by delta T. The top of the fraction stays the same, and the bottom gets smaller. So the calibration factor comes out larger than the true value.
[b04] This surprises a lot of students, because they've memorised "heat loss makes the answer smaller". That's true for some quantities, and false for others. The only reliable method is to trace the affected measurement through the actual formula.
[b05 pause=8] Time for a checkpoint. During a calibration, a student records the heating time as two hundred seconds, when it was really two hundred and forty. Will their calibration factor be too big, or too small? Pause and trace it through.
[b06] It'll be too small. A shorter recorded time gives a smaller calculated E. The measured delta T stays the same, so E over delta T comes out smaller.

## E09S11 | Recap and numerical check
objective: Revisit CF=E/ΔT, time in seconds, matched contents.
onscreen: Calibration formulas and a short numerical checkpoint.

[b01] Let's recap. A calorimeter's calibration factor is the energy needed per degree for the whole setup and its contents. You find it electrically, using E equals V times I times t, with t in seconds. Then the calibration factor is E over delta T. It must be bigger than the water's m c, and it only applies to matching contents. And when something goes wrong, trace it through the formula.
[b02] One last recall question. A heater runs at twelve point zero volts and two point zero zero amps for five minutes, and the temperature rises by six point zero degrees. What's the calibration factor?
[b03] First, five minutes is three hundred seconds. Then E is twelve point zero volts, times two point zero zero amps, times three hundred: seven thousand two hundred joules. Divide that by the temperature rise, six point zero degrees, and the calibration factor is one thousand two hundred joules per degree.
