## E07S02 | Why the smaller number isn't always the limiting reactant
objective: Use recipe batches to show the limiting reactant depends on amount relative to the coefficient.
onscreen: Recipe card (1 CH4 + 2 O2 per batch); 3 CH4 and 4 O2 particle icons grouped into batches; leftover CH4; scaled to moles; n/coefficient comparison.
transitions: Particles snap into batches; leftovers highlighted; then moles replace counts.
checks: 3 CH4, 4 O2 -> 2 batches; O2 limiting; 1 CH4 left. 1.00 mol CH4 vs 1.50 mol O2 -> 1.00 vs 0.75.

[b01] Think of the balanced equation as a recipe for one batch: one methane molecule and two oxygen molecules. Now suppose we have three methane molecules and four oxygen molecules. There are fewer methane molecules. So is methane the one that runs out?
[b02] Let's build some batches. Each one takes one methane and two oxygens. The first batch is fine. So is the second. But now we're out of oxygen, with one methane left over. So oxygen limited the reaction, even though there was more of it.
[b03] That's because of the recipe. Each batch needs twice as much oxygen as methane. So what matters isn't the raw amount of each reactant. It's how many batches each reactant could make on its own. That's its amount divided by its coefficient.
[b04] Now scale this up to moles. Say we have one point zero zero moles of methane and one point five zero moles of oxygen. Methane could make one point zero zero moles of batches, but oxygen could only make zero point seven five. The smaller value of amount divided by coefficient tells us the limiting reactant. Here, that's oxygen.

[b02] First, the amount used. That's the coefficient times the extent. For methane, one times zero point seven five gives zero point seven five moles used. ## E07S06 | Gas mixtures: only the reactive part reacts
objective: Partition gas streams into reactive and non-reactive components; volume fraction as mole fraction under stated assumptions.
onscreen: Air stream bar (21% O2, 79% inert); fuel stream bar (80% CH4, 20% CO2); assumption card; only coloured parts enter the reaction box.
transitions: Each stream splits into labelled parts; non-reactive parts bypass the reaction box.
checks: ideal gases at the same T and p: volume fraction = mole fraction.

[b01] Gases often come as mixtures. Air is about twenty-one percent oxygen by volume. The rest is mostly nitrogen, which doesn't react in these calculations. A fuel stream might be methane mixed with carbon dioxide, which can't burn any further.
[b02] For ideal gases at the same temperature and pressure, equal volumes contain equal amounts. So a volume fraction is also a mole fraction. That's an assumption, and questions usually state it. If the gases are at different conditions, you can't use the fractions like this.
[b03] So split each stream up before you do any stoichiometry. From the air, only the oxygen fraction goes into the reaction. From the fuel, only the methane fraction reacts. The nitrogen and the carbon dioxide just pass through.

## E07S12 | Recap and closing recall
objective: Consolidate the limiting-reactant routine and gas-mixture partitioning.
onscreen: Five-step routine card; recall question; answer; next episode.
transitions: Steps appear one at a time; recall with timer.
checks: 2H2 + O2: 3.0 mol H2, 2.0 mol O2 -> 1.5 vs 2.0, H2 limiting, 0.50 mol O2 left.

[b01] The routine. One: convert everything to moles, using only the reactive part of any mixture. Two: divide each amount by its coefficient; the smallest is limiting, and gives the reaction extent. Three: used equals coefficient times extent. Four: remaining equals initial minus used, never negative. Five: newly formed products equal coefficient times extent. Total products leaving also include any of that component already in the inlet stream.
