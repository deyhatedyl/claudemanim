## E07S02 | Why the smaller number isn't always the limiting reactant
objective: Use recipe batches to show the limiting reactant depends on amount relative to the coefficient.
onscreen: Recipe card (1 CH4 + 2 O2 per batch); 3 CH4 and 4 O2 particle icons grouped into batches; leftover CH4; scaled to moles; n/coefficient comparison.
transitions: Particles snap into batches; leftovers highlighted; then moles replace counts.
checks: 3 CH4, 4 O2 -> 2 batches; O2 limiting; 1 CH4 left. 1.00 mol CH4 vs 1.50 mol O2 -> 1.00 vs 0.75.

[b01] Think of the balanced equation as a recipe for one batch: one methane and two oxygen molecules. Suppose we have three methane molecules and four oxygen molecules. Methane is the smaller number. Is methane the one that runs out?
[b02] Let's build batches. Each batch takes one methane and two oxygens. The first batch: fine. The second batch: fine. Now we're out of oxygen, with one methane left over. So oxygen limited the reaction, even though there was more of it.
[b03] The reason is the recipe. Each batch needs twice as much oxygen as methane. So what matters isn't the raw amount of each reactant. It's how many batches each reactant could make on its own: its amount divided by its coefficient.
[b04] Scale this up to moles. With one point zero zero moles of methane and one point five zero moles of oxygen, methane could make one point zero zero moles of batches, but oxygen only zero point seven five. The smaller value of amount divided by coefficient identifies the limiting reactant: oxygen.

## E07S03 | Initial, used, remaining
objective: Use reaction extent to fill initial, used and remaining trays; check remaining >= 0.
onscreen: Three trays (initial, used, remaining) for CH4, O2, CO2, H2O; extent box; invariant remaining = initial - used; check marks.

[b01] The smaller value, zero point seven five moles, is called the reaction extent: the number of moles of the recipe that actually happen. Everything else follows from it.
[b02] Amount used equals coefficient times extent. Methane: one times zero point seven five, so zero point seven five moles used. Oxygen: two times zero point seven five, so one point five zero moles used, which is all of it, as it should be for the limiting reactant.
[b03] Products form the same way. Carbon dioxide: one times zero point seven five, so zero point seven five moles. Water: two times zero point seven five, so one point five zero moles.
[b04] Finally, remaining equals initial minus used. Methane: one point zero zero minus zero point seven five leaves zero point two five moles in excess. Oxygen: one point five zero minus one point five zero leaves zero. Check: nothing is negative. A negative remaining amount always means the wrong reactant was chosen as limiting.
[b05 pause=10] Quick prediction. This time, zero point two zero moles of methane meets zero point five zero moles of oxygen. Which reactant is limiting, and how much of the other is left? Pause and work it out with amount divided by coefficient.
[b06] Methane: zero point two zero over one is zero point two zero. Oxygen: zero point five zero over two is zero point two five. Methane is smaller, so this time methane is limiting, with an extent of zero point two zero moles. Oxygen used is two times zero point two zero, which is zero point four zero moles, leaving zero point one zero moles of oxygen. The same two reactants can swap roles when the amounts change.

## E07S06 | Gas mixtures: only the reactive part reacts
objective: Partition gas streams into reactive and non-reactive components; volume fraction as mole fraction under stated assumptions.
onscreen: Air stream bar (21% O2, 79% inert); fuel stream bar (80% CH4, 20% CO2); assumption card; only coloured parts enter the reaction box.
transitions: Each stream splits into labelled parts; non-reactive parts bypass the reaction box.
checks: ideal gases at the same T and p: volume fraction = mole fraction.

[b01] Gases often arrive as mixtures. Air is about twenty-one percent oxygen by volume; the rest, mostly nitrogen, doesn't react in these calculations. A fuel stream might be methane mixed with carbon dioxide, which can't burn any further.
[b02] For ideal gases at the same temperature and pressure, equal volumes contain equal amounts. So a volume fraction is also a mole fraction. That's an assumption, and questions usually state it. If the gases are at different conditions, you can't use the fractions this way.
[b03] So split each stream before you do any stoichiometry. From the air, only the oxygen fraction goes into the reaction. From the fuel, only the methane fraction reacts. The nitrogen and the carbon dioxide pass through.

## E07S07 | Gas volumes react in the same ratio
objective: For gases at the same temperature and pressure, volume ratios equal mole ratios; find the oxygen and air needed.
onscreen: 2.00 L propane; C3H8 + 5O2 -> 3CO2 + 4H2O; volume bars 2.00 : 10.0 : 6.00 L; water liquid at SLC; air = 10.0 / 0.210 = 47.6 L; conditions note.

[b01] Here's a shortcut for gases. At the same temperature and pressure, equal volumes of gases contain equal numbers of moles. So gas volumes react in the same ratio as the coefficients, and you don't need to convert to moles at all.
[b02] Propane burns as C three H eight plus five oxygen, forming three carbon dioxide plus four water. So two point zero zero litres of propane needs five times as much oxygen, ten point zero litres, and forms three times as much carbon dioxide, six point zero zero litres, all measured at the same conditions. At SLC the water is a liquid, so it doesn't count as gas volume.
[b03] If the oxygen comes from air that is twenty-one point zero percent oxygen, the air needed is ten point zero divided by zero point two one zero: about forty-seven point six litres of air. The volume shortcut only works for gases, and only when every volume is measured at the same temperature and pressure.

## E07S11 | Recap and closing recall
objective: Consolidate the limiting-reactant routine and gas-mixture partitioning.
onscreen: Five-step routine card; recall question; answer; next episode.
transitions: Steps appear one at a time; recall with timer.
checks: 2H2 + O2: 3.0 mol H2, 2.0 mol O2 -> 1.5 vs 2.0, H2 limiting, 0.50 mol O2 left.

[b01] The routine. One: convert everything to moles, using only the reactive part of any mixture. Two: divide each amount by its coefficient; the smallest is limiting, and gives the reaction extent. Three: used equals coefficient times extent. Four: remaining equals initial minus used, never negative. Five: products formed equal coefficient times extent, plus anything that simply passes through.

[b03] Hydrogen: three point zero over two is one point five. Oxygen: two point zero over one is two point zero. Hydrogen is limiting, with an extent of one point five moles. Oxygen used is one point five moles, so zero point five zero moles of oxygen remains. Next episode: measuring the energy from a fuel, and how efficient heating really is.
